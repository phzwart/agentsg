\begin{synopsis}
Every integer matrix between two Selling-reduced cells of any lattice lies in one set of 6096 matrices. Widened for measured cells, the set gives every candidate reindexing operator within a stated tolerance, and the price of each pseudo-symmetric alternative, calibrated on the PDB, says whether the cell alone can exclude it.
\end{synopsis}

\begin{abstract}
A monoclinic crystal measured twice can reduce once to a cell on $c$ and once to a cell on $a+c$; the integer matrix between the two is not a lattice symmetry. Which integer matrices can relate two reduced cells, and how large must a search be to find them? Every matrix between two Selling-reduced cells of any lattice lies in one fixed set of $6096=48\times127$ matrices with entries up to $\pm3$. For a measured cell the set of reduced cells within a tolerance is finite and is enumerated exactly; below a radius computed for each cell it reduces to a lookup of those 6096 matrices. Small fixed search windows fail in identifiable cases, which the lookup does not. A pseudo-twofold is priced at exactly twice its distance to the lattice it would make exact, and an operator of higher order at most twice, about $5\%$ strain per degree of Le Page obliquity. Across the 206\,172 cells of the PDB, $21\%$ of entries have a higher lattice symmetry within $3^\circ$, and for $7$--$10\%$ the cheapest alternative indexing lies within the strain that separates two crystals of one form, so the cell alone cannot exclude it.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================

Take a primitive monoclinic lattice with $a=30$, $b=40$, $c=50$\,\Ang\ and $\beta=100^\circ$. Its Selling-reduced cell has two right angles between vectors of the reduced superbase, and a measured cell lies slightly to one side or the other of each. One data set therefore reduces to the cell $30$, $40$, $50$\,\Ang, $\beta=100^\circ$, and another, equally correctly, to $30$, $40$, $53.7$\,\Ang, $\beta=113.4^\circ$, built on $a$ and $a+c$ (Section~\ref{sec:example}). The matrix between the two is not a symmetry of the lattice. Data-processing programs find such matrices by trying a list: small integer matrices between the two reduced cells, the matrices that unit-cell similarity searches generate (Grosse-Kunstleve \emph{et al.}, 2002), or the operations of the lattice group found by a symmetry search (Le Page, 1982; Sauter \emph{et al.}, 2004). On ordinary cases these searches succeed, and the question this paper answers is when they are guaranteed to: which integer matrices can relate two reduced cells, how large a search has to be, and how to price the pseudo-symmetric alternatives that a search returns.

The multiplicity has a classical description. A superbase is four lattice vectors summing to zero; it is obtuse, or Selling-reduced, when all six pairwise dot products are non-positive (Selling, 1874). A superbase is obtuse exactly when its partial sums are Voronoi vectors of the lattice (Voronoi, 1908; Conway \& Sloane, 1992), and the obtuse superbase is unique except when a dot product vanishes (Delaunay, 1933; Patterson \& Love, 1957). Kurlin (2022) listed all obtuse superbases of each Voronoi type and derived from them a complete isometry invariant. Delone's 24 sorts classify lattices by the zeros and equalities among the six dot products (Delone \emph{et al.}, 1975; Burzlaff \& Zimmermann, 2006; Andrews \& Bernstein, 2023). The $S^6$ representation (Andrews \emph{et al.}, 2019\emph{b}) writes the same six numbers as a point of a six-dimensional space, with boundary transformations and reflections between the reduced presentations of a lattice. On it rest the distances CS6Dist (Andrews \emph{et al.}, 2019\emph{b}) and NCDist (Andrews \& Bernstein, 2014), the Bravais-type determination of SELLA (Andrews \emph{et al.}, 2023), which projects onto the sorts' subspaces as Andrews \& Bernstein (1988) did in $G^6$, and the lattice matcher of LatticeRepLib (Andrews \& Bernstein, 2026), which finds the presentation of one cell nearest to another. Oishi-Tomiyasu (2012, 2024) gives error-stable Bravais-lattice determination in Selling coordinates with a provably sufficient finite list of basis transforms. The instability of the Niggli cell under error (Andrews \emph{et al.}, 1980; Andrews \& Bernstein, 1988, 2014) is the same multiplicity seen from the Niggli side, and the operators between two settings of one lattice form a coset of its symmetries (Flack, 1987).

This paper adds three things.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A complete operator set.} Every integer matrix between two Selling-reduced cells of any lattice lies in one set $\mathcal U$ of $6096=48\times127$ matrices, with entries up to $\pm3$; the proof is a short lemma and an exhaustive, computer-assisted enumeration (Section~\ref{sec:universal}, Lemma~A1). The entries reach $2$ and $3$ only in identifiable configurations, and a reference whose Niggli cell has three acute angles reaches them (Section~\ref{sec:operators-results}).
\item \emph{The tolerance under which it stays complete.} For a measured cell, the set of superbases that are reduced for some metric within a strain $\epsilon$ is finite and is enumerated exactly (Lemma~A6). Below an adjacency radius computed for each cell, the set is a filter of $\mathcal U$ applied to the reduced cell, so a lookup of the 6096 matrices between two Selling-reduced cells is a complete search (Remark~A7). Reindexing a new cell against a reference widened this way returns every proper operator within the tolerance (Lemma~A8).
\item \emph{The price of pseudo-symmetry, calibrated on the PDB.} A candidate is priced by the affine-invariant distance between the two metrics. A pseudo-twofold costs exactly twice its distance to the lattice it would make exact (Proposition~A9), about $5\%$ per degree of Le Page obliquity. The strain between deposited cells of one crystal form sets the tolerance, and comparing it with the prices of the pseudo-symmetries in the PDB says how often the cell alone cannot decide the indexing (Section~\ref{sec:pdb}).
\end{enumerate}
The guarantee matters where the searches in use have no stated range. On the cases tested here, small-matrix and similarity searches find the true operator as often as the closure does on cells of ordinary shape and error. They fail in the configurations that item~1 identifies, and nothing in their construction says in advance which cells those are.

Section~\ref{sec:background} collects the classical definitions. Section~\ref{sec:closure} defines the closure, works through the monoclinic cell and defines $\mathcal U$. Section~\ref{sec:measured} gives the methods for measured cells, the pricing of pseudo-symmetry and the design of the tests, and states what is proved. Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the results. Proofs are in Appendix~A. Appendix~B describes the distances of a measured cell to the 24 sorts, a by-product of the same computation. The supporting information holds catalogues, the construction of $\mathcal U$ with no lattice, the theory of an absolute band on the conorms, further tables and a list of the computational checks keyed to the sections of this paper.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Superbases, conorms and the metric tensor}
\label{sec:defs}
Selling reduction works with four vectors: the cell vectors $a$, $b$, $c$ and $d=-a-b-c$. The four form a \emph{superbase}, and any three of them are a basis of the lattice. The six negated pair products
\[
p_{da}=-d\cdot a,\quad p_{db}=-d\cdot b,\quad p_{dc}=-d\cdot c,\quad p_{ab}=-a\cdot b,\quad p_{ac}=-a\cdot c,\quad p_{bc}=-b\cdot c
\]
are the \emph{conorms} (Selling, 1874; Conway \& Sloane, 1992). Conorm vectors are always written in this slot order, $da,db,dc,ab,ac,bc$; the Selling parameters of $S^6$ are the same numbers with the opposite sign, in another order. A superbase is \emph{obtuse}, which is to say Selling-reduced, when all six conorms are non-negative. The metric tensor in the basis $(a,b,c)$ is linear in the conorms,
\begin{equation}
\Gmet=\begin{pmatrix}
p_{ab}+p_{ac}+p_{da} & -p_{ab} & -p_{ac}\\
-p_{ab} & p_{ab}+p_{bc}+p_{db} & -p_{bc}\\
-p_{ac} & -p_{bc} & p_{ac}+p_{bc}+p_{dc}
\end{pmatrix},
\label{eq:gram}
\end{equation}
so the conorms are six cell parameters. A zero conorm is a right angle between two of the four vectors; the set of slots that vanish is the \emph{zero pattern}, and the set of conorm vectors with $p\ge0$ and those slots zero is a \emph{face} of the cone of non-negative conorms. A face describes lattices only if its non-zero slots connect the four vectors. Up to relabelling there are six kinds of face: no zero, one zero, two zeros on disjoint pairs, two zeros sharing a vector, and three zeros forming a triangle or a path. There are 38 faces in all. The triangle and the path describe the same lattices, those with three mutually perpendicular lattice vectors, read in different cells, so the six kinds of face are the five Voronoi types (Voronoi, 1908; Conway \& Sloane, 1992). A \emph{frame} is a choice of basis in which a metric tensor or a superbase is written.

\subsection{Flips and swaps}
\label{sec:operators}
Two kinds of integer operator act on a superbase.

\emph{Flips.} A flip at the conorm $p_{ab}$ replaces $a$ by $-a$ and adds the old $a$ to the two other vectors, $(a,b,c,d)\mapsto(-a,\ b,\ c+a,\ d+a)$. The new conorms are $p'_{ab}=-p_{ab}$, $p'_{ac}=p_{da}+p_{ab}$, $p'_{da}=p_{ac}+p_{ab}$, $p'_{bc}=p_{bc}+p_{ab}$, $p'_{db}=p_{db}+p_{ab}$ and $p'_{dc}=p_{dc}-p_{ab}$. Selling reduction applies flips at negative conorms until none remain. When $p_{ab}$ is exactly zero the flip exchanges the slots $ac$ and $da$ and carries one obtuse superbase to another of the same lattice, which is why the reduced cell of a lattice with a right angle is not unique. In the basis $(a,b,c)$ each flip is a fixed $3\times3$ integer matrix of determinant $-1$ that is its own inverse (Table~\ref{tab:flips}). A word of flips such as $ab.bc.db$ is applied left to right, each flip being read in the labelling current at that step, and its matrix is $\mathbf F_{ab}\mathbf F_{bc}\mathbf F_{db}$, columns being the new basis vectors in the old basis.

\emph{Swaps.} A superbase is an unordered set of four vectors up to a common sign, so one reduced cell can be written in $4!\times2=48$ ways. The 48 reorderings, with or without a change of the common sign, are the swaps. They change which vectors are called $a,b,c$ and nothing else. Negating a single basis vector is not a swap: it does not preserve $a+b+c+d=0$.

\emph{In $S^6$.} A flip is a boundary transformation of $S^6$ (Andrews \emph{et al.}, 2019\emph{b}), written here on the basis rather than on the six parameters. A swap acts on the six Selling parameters as one of the 24 reflections of $S^6$; the common sign change acts trivially on dot products, so the 48 swaps give 24 reflections. The images of a cell under the boundary transformations and reflections that stay in the all-non-positive orthant of $S^6$ are its reduced presentations, the objects of the closure defined below.

\subsection{Delone sorts and the root invariant}
\label{sec:sorts}
Delone (published as Delaunay, 1933) classified lattices into 24 sorts by the pattern of zeros and equalities among the reduced conorms. Each sort lies in one Bravais type, and some Bravais types comprise several sorts (Delone \emph{et al.}, 1975; Burzlaff \& Zimmermann, 1985, 2006). We use the symbols C1 to A3 and the patterns of Andrews \& Bernstein (2023) and SELLA (Andrews \emph{et al.}, 2023), read in the slot order of Section~\ref{sec:defs}; the correspondence with their slot order is given in the supporting information. The sorts are the Selling counterpart of the 44 Niggli lattice characters (Niggli, 1928; de Wolff \& Gruber, 1991). They split a Bravais type by the shape of the reduced cell, not by its symmetry. A zero conorm restricts the metric without adding a symmetry: one right angle between two superbase vectors gives no twofold, so the triclinic sorts A2 and A3, with one and two zeros, are as triclinic as A1.

All obtuse superbases of a lattice carry the same six conorm values as a multiset, since a flip at a zero conorm only exchanges two slots. Which slots hold which value is not invariant: in the monoclinic example the flip at $ab$ moves $p_{ac}$ into the slot $da$. Kurlin (2022) normalises the square roots of the conorms, by a rule that depends on the Voronoi type, to the root invariant, a complete isometry invariant of the lattice. The invariant identifies a lattice; the closure records how its reduced cells are related.

\section{The Selling closure}
\label{sec:closure}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Definition}
\label{sec:groupoid-def}
A lattice has no single preferred reduced cell. It has a finite set of obtuse superbases, and two kinds of integer matrix relate them: a symmetry maps a setting to itself and leaves its metric tensor unchanged, and a reindexing maps one setting to another, rewriting the metric tensor in the new basis without changing the lattice. A group holds only the first kind. A \emph{groupoid} holds both: it is a collection of objects with invertible arrows between them, in which two arrows can be composed only when the first ends where the second starts (Brandt, 1927; Higgins, 1971).

A \emph{labelling} of an obtuse superbase is a choice of which three of its vectors, in which order and with which common sign, are called $a,b,c$; each obtuse superbase has 48 labellings, joined by swaps. Each labelling gives a basis and so a metric tensor. The \emph{Selling closure} $\Cl(\Gmet)$ of a lattice has as objects these metric tensors, one for each labelling of each obtuse superbase, two labellings that a symmetry carries into each other giving the same tensor. Its arrows from $\Gmet$ to $\Gmet'$ are the unimodular matrices $\Pop$ with $\Pop^{\mathsf T}\Gmet\Pop=\Gmet'$, composed by matrix multiplication; it is the full subgroupoid, on these finitely many objects, of the action of $GL_3(\mathbb Z)$ on positive definite matrices. The point group acts freely on labellings, since a symmetry that fixes a labelled basis is the identity, so a lattice with $n$ obtuse superbases has $48n/|\text{point group}|$ objects. Elsewhere in the paper a \emph{superbase} is the unlabelled set of four vectors, and the closure is counted by its $n$ superbases. Three facts organise it.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The arrows from an object to itself are the point group.} $\{\Pop:\Pop^{\mathsf T}\Gmet\Pop=\Gmet\}$ is the lattice point group written in that basis (Himes \& Mighell, 1987).
\item \emph{The arrows between two objects are one coset of the point group.} If $\Pop_0$ is one arrow from $\Gmet$ to $\Gmet'$, every arrow between them is $g\Pop_0$ with $g$ a symmetry of $\Gmet$ (Flack, 1987).
\item \emph{The arrows are known in advance.} A lattice has $n=1$, $2$, $3$, $6$ or $16$ obtuse superbases according to its zero pattern (Kurlin, 2022; Lemma~A1 gives the counts independently). Every arrow is a word of flips at zero conorms followed by a swap (Lemma~A3), and every arrow lies in one fixed set $\mathcal U$ of integer matrices (Section~\ref{sec:universal}).
\end{enumerate}
The closure is connected, and a connected groupoid is equivalent to the group of arrows at any one object, so facts 1 and 2 hold for any connected groupoid. What is particular to the closure is how the point group acts on the $n$ superbases, on which $-\mathbf I$ acts trivially. The stabiliser of a superbase $F_0$ is the set of symmetries that merely relabel it, its \emph{visible} symmetries. Its orbit is the set of superbases of the same \emph{shape}, those whose conorm vectors agree with $F_0$'s up to the slot permutation induced by a swap. By the orbit--stabiliser theorem the order of the point group is the number of visible symmetries times the number of superbases of $F_0$'s shape, and the number of shapes is the number of orbits. Every symmetry that is not visible carries the reduced cell to a different reduced cell, and no symmetry relates cells of different shape. A reduced cell here is always an obtuse superbase; a Niggli cell of type II (no acute angle) is one, and a Niggli cell of type I (three acute angles) is not.

\subsection{One monoclinic cell}
\label{sec:example}
Take $a=30$, $b=40$, $c=50$\,\Ang, $\beta=100^\circ$. Its reduced superbase is $M_0=(d,a,b,c)$ with conorms
\[
p_{da}=639.5,\quad p_{db}=1600.0,\quad p_{dc}=2239.5,\quad p_{ab}=0,\quad p_{ac}=260.5,\quad p_{bc}=0\ \Ang^2 .
\]
The two zeros are the right angles $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ at the twofold axis $b$, present in every primitive monoclinic lattice. The closure has six superbases (Table~\ref{tab:mono}). Each has two zero conorms, each flip at a zero leads to a neighbour, and the six form a ring $M_0$--$M_1$--$M_5$--$M_4$--$M_3$--$M_2$--$M_0$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ring}). They fall into three shapes. $\{M_0,M_4\}$ is $30$, $40$, $50$\,\Ang, $\beta=100^\circ$; $\{M_1,M_3\}$ is $30$, $40$, $53.7$\,\Ang, $\beta=113.4^\circ$, the cell on $a$ and $a+c$; $\{M_2,M_5\}$ is the third cell choice, on $c$ and $a+c$. These are the three cell choices of \emph{International Tables} (Hahn, 2006, Section~2.2.16).

The point group is $2/m$, and the last column of Table~\ref{tab:mono} shows how its four operations act on the six superbases. The identity and the inversion are swaps of $M_0$: they relabel it. The twofold sends $(a,b,c)\mapsto(-a,b,-c)$ and so carries the four vectors $(-a-b-c,a,b,c)$ to $(a-b+c,-a,b,-c)$, which are the vectors of $M_4$, three flips away. As an arrow of the closure each symmetry is a loop at the metric tensor of $M_0$; as a word in flips and swaps the twofold is the flips $ab$, $bc$, $db$ --- the path $M_0\to M_1\to M_5\to M_4$ --- followed by the swap that renames the vectors of $M_4$ so that its metric tensor is that of $M_0$, and the mirror is the same flips with that swap composed with the change of common sign. Half of the point group is therefore invisible in the reduced cell: the stabiliser of $M_0$ has two elements, and the orbit of $M_0$ is $\{M_0,M_4\}$, as the count $4=2\times2$ requires. ``Visible'' means only that an operation relabels the reduced superbase; a search over lattice directions, such as Le Page's, finds the twofold either way. Two measurements that reduce to $M_0$ and to $M_1$ report different cell parameters and are related by no symmetry, only by the flip at $ab$.

\subsection{The universal operator set}
\label{sec:universal}
The arrows can be characterised with no lattice at all. An integer matrix $\Wop$ acts on the six conorms by a fixed integer $6\times6$ matrix, and $\Wop$ is an arrow of the closure of every lattice on a face exactly when that matrix maps the face into the cone of non-negative conorms, a sign condition on its entries (supporting information, Section~S3). The union over the 38 faces is the set $\mathcal U$. It has $6096=48\times127$ elements with entries in $\{-3,\dots,3\}$ and is closed under inversion. By Lemma~A1 and the exhaustive enumeration of its window, it contains every arrow between two reduced cells of any lattice. The superbases $\mathcal U F_0$, 127 up to relabelling, are all the superbases that can be obtuse together with $F_0$ for some metric. This set carries the completeness statements of Section~\ref{sec:measured}, and its entries say how large a search over integer matrices has to be (Section~\ref{sec:operators-results}).

\section{Measured cells: methods}
\label{sec:measured}
%==============================================================================

A measured cell is never exactly on a zero conorm: a right angle is measured as $90.25^\circ$, and the conorm is a small number of either sign. The flips that joined the superbases of Table~\ref{tab:mono} now lead to superbases with a slightly negative conorm, Selling reduction rejects them, and the exact closure of the measured metric has one superbase instead of six. The closure has to be widened.

\subsection{Two measures of mismatch}
\label{sec:strain}
Two measures are used, both the same in every basis. The \emph{strain} of a perturbation $\Delta\Gmet$ of a metric $\Gmet$ is
\begin{equation}
\|\Delta\Gmet\|_{\Gmet}=\|\Gmet^{-1/2}\,\Delta\Gmet\,\Gmet^{-1/2}\|_F .
\label{eq:strain}
\end{equation}
It is unchanged by a change of basis, since $\Gmet\mapsto\Pop^{\mathsf T}\Gmet\Pop$ and $\Delta\Gmet\mapsto\Pop^{\mathsf T}\Delta\Gmet\Pop$ leave it as it is, and by a common scale of the cell. A perturbation of strain $\epsilon$ changes the dot product of any two lattice vectors by at most $\epsilon|u||v|$ (Proposition~A4). The strain measures how far a metric must move to make a superbase obtuse, a convex problem in its six conorms. The \emph{geodesic distance} between two metrics is
\begin{equation}
\dist(\Gmet_1,\Gmet_2)=\Bigl(\sum_i \ln^2\lambda_i\Bigr)^{1/2},
\label{eq:geo}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_i$ the eigenvalues of $\Gmet_1^{-1/2}\Gmet_2\Gmet_1^{-1/2}$, the affine-invariant distance on positive definite matrices (Pennec \emph{et al.}, 2006). It agrees with the strain to first order, it is a true distance (symmetric, with the triangle inequality), and every change of basis is an isometry for it. It measures how far apart two cells are, and it prices operators.

Table~\ref{tab:tol} converts the classical tolerances. For Le Page's twofold criterion (Le Page, 1982) the conversion is exact: a twofold whose obliquity, the angle between a lattice row and the nearest reciprocal row, is $\delta$ sits at a geodesic distance $\sqrt2\,\mathrm{artanh}(\sin\delta)$ from the metrics it would leave invariant, and its price is twice that (Proposition~A9). One degree of obliquity is a price of $4.9\%$, and Le Page's $3^\circ$ a price of $14.8\%$, for every twofold separately.

\subsection{The widened set}
\label{sec:widened}
The \emph{widened closure} of a metric $\Gmet$ at strain $\epsilon$ is
\begin{equation}
\Se(\Gmet)=\bigl\{\,F\;:\;F\ \text{is obtuse for some metric within strain}\ \epsilon\ \text{of}\ \Gmet\,\bigr\}.
\label{eq:def}
\end{equation}
Its superbases are the reduced cells of every lattice consistent with the measurement at that tolerance. Each superbase has a \emph{depth}, the strain from $\Gmet$ to the nearest metric that makes it obtuse, and belongs to $\Se(\Gmet)$ when its depth is at most $\epsilon$. Equation~(\ref{eq:def}) names no starting cell, order of operations or frame, so $\Se(\Gmet)$ is the same whoever computes it. Each operator $\Pop$ from a metric $\Gmet_1$ to a metric $\Gmet_2$ has a \emph{price}, the geodesic distance between $\Gmet_2$ and $s\Pop^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_1\Pop$ minimised over a common scale $s$, which removes the trace of the logarithm in equation~(\ref{eq:geo}). The price is zero for every arrow of an exact closure, and for a unimodular self-map the scale changes nothing.

\emph{It is finite.} The lattice vectors modulo twice the lattice fall into eight \emph{parity classes}, seven of which do not contain the zero vector; the seven partial sums of an obtuse superbase are shortest vectors of those classes. With $\mu_c$ the squared length of the shortest vector of class $c$, every vector of a superbase in $\Se(\Gmet)$ lies in a ball of squared radius $\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\mu_c$ (Lemma~A6), so $\Se(\Gmet)$ is enumerated exactly, with no window of coordinates to assume.

\emph{Widening one side suffices.} If an operator $\Pop$ from a reference to a new cell has price at most $\epsilon$, the new cell's reduced superbase, carried back by $\Pop$, lies in the reference's widened closure at strain $e^\epsilon-1$ (Lemma~A8). So only the reference is widened, and the new cell is reduced once.

\subsection{Computing the widened set}
\label{sec:snap}
For a measured cell and a strain $\epsilon<\frac14$: Selling-reduce the cell to a superbase $F_0$; enumerate the lattice vectors in the seven balls of Lemma~A6 and form the superbases they make; keep those of depth at most $\epsilon$, with each one's depth and the operator $\Wop$ from $F_0$ to it. This is exact for every cell. Walking from the reduced cell, flipping wherever a conorm is within a band of zero, is not: a member can be reachable only through a non-member, when two small conorms lie at, say, $1.2$ and $0.5$ times the band and only the second of two flips returns into it. We call such a member a \emph{difference-wall member}, since it lies across the wall where the two small conorms are equal (supporting information, Table~S3).

\emph{Layers and the lookup.} Call two superbases adjacent when some metric makes both obtuse. The superbases adjacent to $F_0$ are the first layer $\Lay_1=\mathcal U F_0$, and $\Lay_k=\mathcal U\Lay_{k-1}$. The \emph{adjacency radius} $r_k(\Gmet)$ is the strain from $\Gmet$ to the nearest metric for which a superbase outside $\Lay_k$ is obtuse; the implementation computes it up to $25\%$. If $\epsilon<r_1$, the widened set is the set of members of $\Lay_1$ of depth at most $\epsilon$ (Remark~A7). It follows that, for two cells within price $\epsilon$ of each other with $e^\epsilon-1<r_1$ of the reference, trying the 6096 matrices of $\mathcal U$ between the two Selling-reduced cells finds every operator within $\epsilon$. This lookup is a small-matrix search with a stated range. Computing $r_1$ costs as much as the exact enumeration, so the lookup is certified only where $r_1$ is known; Section~\ref{sec:complete} reports it for a range of cells.

\emph{Ties take no band.} Every sort lies on one zero pattern, and every symmetry of every lattice on that pattern is one of its arrows (Lemma~A3), so a near-equality --- the $a\approx b$ of a pseudo-tetragonal cell --- never adds a superbase: the operator that would be its symmetry is already enumerated, and its price decides whether it is accepted.

\subsection{Reindexing}
\label{sec:reindex-method}
A reference $R$ is stored as its widened closure, the operators $\Wop_k$ from its reduced cell to the members. A new cell $N$ is reduced once, to a basis $B_N$ in its own coordinates; $B_R$ is the reduction matrix of the reference. For each stored $\Wop_k$ and each of the 48 swaps $\mathbf Q$ the candidate operator from the reference's input basis to the new cell's is
\begin{equation}
\Pop=B_R\Wop_k\mathbf Q\,B_N^{-1},
\label{eq:candidate}
\end{equation}
so that $(a_N\,b_N\,c_N)=(a_R\,b_R\,c_R)\Pop$, $\Gmet_N\approx\Pop^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_R\Pop$, and Miller indices transform as $h_N=\Pop^{\mathsf T}h_R$. Candidates of price at most the band $\epsilon$ are returned; the reference is widened at $e^\epsilon-1$, so the true operator is among them whenever its price is at most $\epsilon$ (Lemma~A8). Three conventions complete the procedure. Only operators of positive determinant are returned: cell parameters carry no hand, so the convention is that both bases are right-handed, and an inverted geometry must be caught before the cell reaches this step. A fitted scale that changes the edge lengths by more than $10\%$ is rejected, and two primitive cells whose volumes differ by a factor of $1.6$ or more are refused with a message, since no unimodular operator relates them; a wrong centring symbol, a doubled axis or an obverse--reverse relation, which is a relation between two different lattices, lands here. A centred reference is first taken to a primitive cell and the centring matrix composed with the operator found, which is then rational in the conventional basis.

\emph{Cosets and classes.} Let $H_\epsilon$ be the proper unimodular self-maps of the reference of price at most $\epsilon$, and $p_0$ the price of the true operator $\Pop_0$. Because the price is a distance, every candidate is $h\Pop_0$ with $h\in H_{\epsilon+p_0}$, and every $h\in H_{\epsilon-p_0}$ gives a candidate; if no self-map has a price in $(\epsilon,2\epsilon]$, $H_\epsilon$ is a group, and if none has a price in $(\epsilon-p_0,\epsilon+p_0]$, the candidates are exactly the coset $H_\epsilon\Pop_0$ (Proposition~A10). Two candidates that differ on the left by an element of the reference's Laue group $\Laue$ index the crystal the same way, so the distinct indexings are the classes of the proper part $\Laue^+$ of $\Laue$ among the candidates. For anomalous data from an acentric crystal the point group replaces the Laue group. The guarantee is that the true operator is among the candidates; the price orders the classes but does not pick the true member, which is a matter for the intensities.

\subsection{Pricing pseudo-symmetry, and choosing the band}
\label{sec:pricing}
A symmetry $g$ of a nearby lattice enters $H_\epsilon$ when its price is at most $\epsilon$. For a twofold the price is exactly twice the geodesic distance from the measured metric to the metrics $V_g$ that $g$ leaves invariant; for a rotation of order $n$ the price is at most twice that distance, and to first order near $V_g$ the ratio is $\sqrt3$ for a threefold, between $\sqrt2$ and $2$ for a fourfold and between $1$ and $\sqrt3$ for a sixfold (Proposition~A9). One threshold on the price of each operator therefore serves every sort, and Le Page's $3^\circ$ is a price of $14.8\%$ for each twofold.

The band is a tolerance on how much two measurements of one crystal may differ. For macromolecular crystals that difference is dominated by non-isomorphism, not by the measurement error that the standard uncertainties of a cell describe. We measure non-isomorphism in the PDB (Section~\ref{sec:design}) and set the band from its upper percentiles. A candidate's price is then reported as a percentile of that distribution, which reads directly: a class priced at the median non-isomorphism is as close to the reference as a typical second crystal of the same form, and the metric cannot exclude it. For small-molecule data, whose cells are measured precisely and rarely non-isomorphous, a band of a few times the strain implied by the esds is used instead (\texttt{strain\_from\_esds} in the implementation).

\subsection{Tests}
\label{sec:design}
\emph{Constructed cases.} The benchmark (supporting information, Section~S2) has 780 primitive cells with a random metric error and a random proper reindexing, built where the closure predicts trouble: 75 measured in another shape of the same lattice, 660 a small distance from a specialisation, 20 whose only reduced superbase is a difference-wall member, 16 needles and plates and 9 of the boundary sorts. Its errors are small (median strain $0.1\%$). The stress family puts independent errors on both cells of 60 references, scaled so that the true operator's price is $0.5$ and $0.9$ times the band. The window family probes the mechanisms of Section~\ref{sec:operators-results}: 300 lattices with two right angles sharing a vector or three right angles, each measured twice with an error of $0.1$ to $2\%$, the reference given as its Niggli cell and the new cell in a random basis, and 60 lattices whose closure has a member needing an entry of $3$, measured in that member. Seven methods are scored: the closure; the lookup of $\mathcal U$; searches over every unimodular matrix with entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$ (6960 matrices) and $\{-2,\dots,2\}$ between the two Selling-reduced cells; cctbx \texttt{similarity\_\allowbreak transformations} between the two Niggli cells (Grosse-Kunstleve \emph{et al.}, 2002) with generator ranges 1 and 2; and a pipeline that runs the cctbx lattice-symmetry analysis (Sauter \emph{et al.}, 2004) on both cells and returns every operation of the reference's highest lattice group between the two standard settings. Every method's candidates are priced the same way and kept within the band. On the window family the LatticeRepLib lattice matcher (\texttt{CmdLM}; Andrews \& Bernstein, 2026) is run as well; it returns the presentation of the new cell nearest to the reference, not an operator, and is scored by whether that presentation lies within the band.

\emph{The PDB.} The snapshot of 206\,214 deposited cells (Zenodo record 22986222; see Data availability) is used twice. Non-isomorphism is measured between entries of one crystal form, taken as entries with the same space group whose identifiers are adjacent and share their first three characters, so that most pairs come from one deposition series; both cells are in the standard setting of the space group, so the identity is the operator, and pairs above $20\%$ are counted as different forms. Pseudo-symmetry is measured by running the cctbx lattice-symmetry analysis at $3^\circ$ on the primitive Niggli cell of every entry, recording the highest lattice group, its obliquity and the Delone sort of the idealised lattice, and pricing every proper operator of that group that is not a symmetry of the reported space group. Deposited cells are refined in the reported space group, so they satisfy its symmetry exactly; what the analysis measures is how far real crystals lie from a higher lattice, not measurement error.

Every entry of Table~\ref{tab:sorts} is the result of this linear algebra on integer matrices. \subsection{How large a search must be}
\label{sec:operators-results}

When does reduction put a zero on $d$? The entries are read in the basis of the first cell, so the question is about the reference, and Selling reduction started from its Niggli cell answers by the Niggli type. For a lattice near primitive orthorhombic the reason is a sign. The superbases whose three zeros avoid $d$ are the orthogonal cells $(\pm a,\pm b,\pm c)$, and such a cell is obtuse for the measured metric only if $a\cdot b$, $a\cdot c$ and $b\cdot c$ are all non-positive. Negating basis vectors cannot change the sign of their product. A type~II Niggli cell (all three non-positive) is therefore already obtuse, with its zeros off $d$. A type~I cell (all three positive) cannot be made obtuse by signs, so it reduces to a face-diagonal cell, whose three zeros form a path through $d$. For a lattice near primitive monoclinic the same holds less sharply. A type~II Niggli cell is already an obtuse superbase, and its right angles stay on the slots $ab$, $ac$, $bc$: in 519 of 519 measured near-mP cells no zero landed on $d$. A type~I Niggli cell, with three acute angles, cannot be made obtuse by sign changes, reduction flips four times, and in 614 of 681 cells the two near-zero conorms ended on slots of $d$. Which type a measurement gets is decided by the signs of its small errors, so about half of all measurements of a near-monoclinic or near-orthorhombic lattice are type~I. From a type~I reference, an operator to another measurement of the lattice can need an entry of $2$ in the bases that reduction returns; from a type~II reference it never does, whatever the Niggli type of the new cell.

\subsection{Reindexing}
\label{sec:reindex}
\emph{Indexing classes.} Table~\ref{tab:classes} and Fig.~\ref{fig:classes} give the classes for six references against the same crystal measured again with a $0.1\%$ error in the length scale, which the scale fit absorbs, and a relabelling. \emph{What the metric does not decide.} The cheapest candidate lies in the true operator's coset of the exact lattice symmetries in 742 of the 780 benchmark cases. \subsection{Pseudo-symmetry in the PDB}
\label{sec:pdb}
\emph{Non-isomorphism.} Between deposited cells of one crystal form (45\,222 pairs) the price after the scale fit has a median of $0.98\%$, a 90th percentile of $4.2\%$ and a 95th of $6.7\%$; without the scale fit the median is $1.6\%$. This is the scale a reindexing band has to cover for macromolecular data: about $4\%$ covers nine pairs in ten and $7\%$ nineteen in twenty. The standard uncertainties of a single cell describe its measurement error alone and do not reach this scale.

\emph{Pseudo-symmetry.} Le Page's analysis at $3^\circ$ finds a higher lattice symmetry than the reported one for 43\,232 of the 206\,172 entries analysed ($21\%$); for 1\,189 ($0.6\%$) the extra symmetry is exact, the metric of a merohedral crystal. For the other 42\,043 the largest price equals $2\sqrt2\,\mathrm{artanh}(\sin\delta)$ for the group's largest obliquity $\delta$, the relation of Proposition~A9 (ratio $1.000$ at the median and the 10th and 90th percentiles), and the cheapest operator has a median price of $6.7\%$. Fig.~\ref{fig:pdb} sets this distribution against non-isomorphism: the cheapest alternative lies within the 90th percentile of non-isomorphism for $35\%$ of the pseudo-symmetric entries and within the 95th for $50\%$. Over all entries, $7.2\%$ have a priced alternative within $4.2\%$ and $10.2\%$ within $6.7\%$. For those the alternative indexing is as close to the reference as a second crystal of the same form, and only the intensities can exclude it.

\emph{Where.} The share depends on the reported type and on the transition (Table~\ref{tab:pdb}). Monoclinic and triclinic entries carry most of it: $18\%$ of the 35\,364 mP entries and $16\%$ of the 8\,522 aP entries have an alternative within $4.2\%$, against $7\%$ of oP and $1\%$ of tP entries. Among the transitions, mP to hP is nearly always undecidable from the cell (median price $0.71\%$, $91\%$ within $4.2\%$): these are hexagonal metrics reported in a monoclinic group, the classic pseudo-merohedral twinning case. The median price of the cheapest alternative is $6$--$7.5\%$ for most transitions, so the sort decides how often a pseudo-symmetry occurs, not what it costs (supporting information, Table~S8).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%==============================================================================

\emph{A guarantee where searches have none.} The searches in use find the true operator on ordinary cells, and in the comparison of Section~\ref{sec:reindex} the closure finds no more than the cctbx similarity searches or the lattice matcher of LatticeRepLib; it finds more than the fixed windows and the lattice-symmetry pipeline only in the configurations below. What it adds is a statement of range. The lookup of the 6096 matrices of $\mathcal U$ between the two Selling-reduced cells is complete below the adjacency radius of the reference, which is above $25\%$ for cells of ordinary shape and is computed for every cell; beyond it the exact enumeration takes over. A fixed window of entries, or the operations of one lattice group, carries no such statement, and Section~\ref{sec:operators-results} identifies where each runs out: entries of $3$ arise on a path of three zeros, and entries of $2$ with zeros sharing a vector on $d$; a near-monoclinic or near-orthorhombic reference reaches such a face when its Niggli cell is type~I, and from such references the $\{-1,0,1\}$ window misses about one operator in six.

\emph{Pseudo-symmetry and non-isomorphism.} The PDB analysis turns a tolerance into a decision. The price of a pseudo-symmetric alternative is a geodesic distance and can be compared directly with the distance between two crystals of one form. Where the alternative costs less than ordinary non-isomorphism, as for 7--10\% of PDB entries at the 90th and 95th percentiles, no metric criterion can exclude it and the intensities must decide, as in twinning and indexing-ambiguity tests (Yeates, 1997; Evans, 2006, 2011; Brehm \& Diederichs, 2014; Gildea \& Winter, 2018). Where it costs more, the cell alone ranks it below the true indexing. The rule of thumb, about $5\%$ of price per degree of Le Page obliquity, makes the two scales comparable: the 90th percentile of non-isomorphism is about $0.85^\circ$, and Le Page's $3^\circ$, a tolerance for assigning a Bravais lattice, is about three times wider than the band needed for reindexing. One threshold per operator serves every Delone sort, since the price of a pseudo-symmetry depends on its obliquity and not on the sort; the sort decides how often each kind of pseudo-symmetry occurs (Table~S8).

\emph{The Niggli cell.} The instability of the Niggli cell (Andrews \emph{et al.}, 1980; Andrews \& Bernstein, 1988, 2014) is the same phenomenon in part. At a zero scalar product the Niggli cell changes between type~I and type~II, and the closure handles that boundary as it handles any zero conorm. At a tie such as $a=b$, and at Niggli's special conditions, the Niggli basis changes while the Selling-reduced superbase does not: such changes are relabellings of one superbase, as Andrews \emph{et al.} (2019\emph{a}) observed in comparing the two reductions. In the benchmark the measured cell's Niggli cell differs from the reference's in 45 of 780 cases and is the same cell in another basis in 125 more (supporting information, Section~S2). Tolerant Niggli reduction (K\v{r}iv\'y \& Gruber, 1976; Grosse-Kunstleve \emph{et al.}, 2004) returns one of these cells, not the set.

\emph{Relation to $S^6$, SELLA, the lattice matcher and BLDConograph.} The flips and swaps are the boundary transformations and reflections of $S^6$ (Andrews \emph{et al.}, 2019\emph{b}), and the closure of a lattice is the set of its images under those maps that stay in the reduced orthant. The LatticeRepLib lattice matcher uses the same maps to find the presentation of a cell nearest to a reference, and finds it in the window family as the closure does (Section~\ref{sec:reindex}); it returns a cell rather than the operator and the classes, and it carries no completeness statement. CS6Dist and NCDist are metrics on lattice space, suitable for database search, and return a distance, not an operator. Oishi-Tomiyasu (2012, 2024) bounds the basis transforms needed for Bravais-lattice determination under an error bound; the layers and adjacency radii here do the same for reindexing, with the radius computed for each cell. Appendix~B compares the distances of a cell to the 24 sorts with SELLA.

\emph{Scope.} ``Complete'' in this paper means: every proper unimodular operator whose price is at most the band is among the candidates, for a band the user supplies. The enumeration is exact below a strain of $25\%$, and the lookup of $\mathcal U$ below the adjacency radius. Sub- and superlattice relations (a doubled axis, an obverse--reverse relation between two rhombohedral lattices, determinant $\pm2$ or more) are outside the closure and are refused with a message; the choice within a class is left to the intensities. The PDB measurements describe deposited cells, refined with their reported symmetry imposed, and the non-isomorphism proxy, adjacent identifiers of one space group, mixes in different proteins and crystal forms at the tail, so its upper percentiles are upper estimates.

\section{Conclusions}
%==============================================================================

Every integer matrix between two Selling-reduced cells of any lattice lies in one set of 6096 matrices with entries up to $\pm3$. For a measured cell the reduced cells within a strain tolerance are finite and enumerated exactly, and below a radius computed for each cell a lookup of those 6096 matrices between the two reduced cells is a complete search. Smaller fixed windows fail in configurations that can be named: a reference whose Niggli cell is type~I, with three acute angles, near a lattice with right angles, which reduction carries onto a face with zeros on $d$. Prices of candidate operators are geodesic distances, so their coset structure holds exactly, and a pseudo-twofold costs twice its distance to the lattice it would make exact, about $5\%$ per degree of Le Page obliquity. In practice: store each reference as its widened closure; Selling-reduce each new data set once; keep the proper candidates within a band set by non-isomorphism, about $4$--$7\%$ for macromolecular crystals; group them by the Laue group; and report each class's price against the non-isomorphism distribution. For about one PDB entry in ten the alternative indexing is then as close as a second crystal of the same form, and the intensities have to decide.

\section{Proofs}

\emph{Terms.} Write a lattice vector in any basis, $x=n_1v_1+n_2v_2+n_3v_3$ with integer $n_i$, and record whether each coordinate is even or odd. This sorts the lattice into eight \emph{parity classes}, the classes of $\Lambda/2\Lambda$; two vectors are in the same class exactly when their difference is twice a lattice vector, so the classes do not depend on the basis. One class is the doubled vectors; the other seven are used here. A \emph{partial sum} of a superbase is the sum of some of its four vectors; there are seven up to sign, one in each of the seven classes. A superbase is obtuse exactly when each of its partial sums is a shortest vector of its parity class, that is, a Voronoi vector of the lattice (Voronoi, 1908; Conway \& Sloane, 1992; Kurlin, 2022); at a zero conorm the partial sums are Voronoi vectors that are not strict. The \emph{excess} $e_c(F)=|v_c(F)|^2-\mu_c$ of a superbase $F$ in class $c$ is the squared length of its partial sum in that class minus the squared length $\mu_c$ of the shortest vector of the class. A partial sum is written $w=\sum s_iv_i$ with $s\in\{0,1\}^4$; a vector of the same class has coordinates $s+2k$ with $k$ integer. Every lattice vector $x=\sum c_iv_i$ satisfies Selling's identity
\begin{equation}
|x|^2=\sum_{i<j}p_{ij}\,(c_i-c_j)^2 ,
\label{eq:selling}
\end{equation}
whatever the signs of the conorms, because the four vectors sum to zero.

\emph{Lemma A1 (window).} Let $F_0$ and $F$ be obtuse superbases of the same lattice. Then every vector of $F$ has superbase coordinates $s_i+2k_i$ relative to $F_0$, with $s\in\{0,1\}^4$, $k_0=0$ and $|k_i|\le3$. \emph{Proof.} The non-zero conorms of $F_0$ connect its four vectors, since otherwise a partial sum would have length zero by equation~(\ref{eq:selling}). Every partial sum of $F$ is a shortest vector of its parity class, as is the partial sum of $F_0$ in that class, so their excess vanishes; by Lemma~A2 the excess is $4\sum p^0_{ij}\gamma_{ij}$ with every term non-negative, so $\gamma_{ij}=0$ on every non-zero slot, i.e.\ $|k_i-k_j|\le1$ along a connected graph on four vertices, and with $k_0=0$ every $|k_i|\le3$.\hfill$\square$ Enumerating, over all 38 faces, the superbases of such vectors whose six pairwise products are non-positive on the whole face gives 1, 2, 3, 6 and 16 superbases for no zero, one zero, two disjoint zeros, two zeros sharing a vector and three zeros (both placements), independently of Kurlin's lists. In the basis of $F_0$ their entries are in $\{-1,0,1\}$ when the zero conorms avoid $d$, and up to $3$ otherwise (Table~\ref{tab:entries}): the conorms $(0,0,3,2,0,5)$ have the obtuse basis $(-3a-2b-c,\ a,\ a+b)$. Every member lies in $\mathcal U$ in each of its 48 labellings, which is the computer-assisted proof that $\mathcal U$ contains every arrow, and every member has a labelling with entries in $[-2,2]$.

\emph{Lemma A2 (class excess identity).} Let $F$ be any superbase, $w=\sum s_iv_i$ a partial sum and $w'$ a vector of its parity class with coordinates $s+2k$. Then $|w|^2-|w'|^2=-4\sum p_{ij}\gamma_{ij}$, with $\gamma_{ij}=\Delta k(\Delta k+\Delta s)$, $\Delta k=k_i-k_j$, $\Delta s=s_i-s_j$; each $\gamma_{ij}$ is a non-negative integer, zero exactly when $\Delta k\in\{0,-\Delta s\}$. \emph{Proof.} Equation~(\ref{eq:selling}), $(\Delta s+2\Delta k)^2-\Delta s^2=4\Delta k(\Delta k+\Delta s)$, and $t(t+u)\ge0$ for integers $t$ and $u\in\{-1,0,1\}$.\hfill$\square$

\emph{Lemma A3 (generation).} Every arrow of the closure, and every symmetry, is a word of flips at zero conorms followed by a swap. \emph{Proof.} Let $F$ and $F'$ be obtuse superbases of a lattice with metric $\Gmet$. Add a small positive amount, in the frame of $F$, to each conorm of $F$ that vanishes under $\Gmet$; this gives a metric $\Gmet'$ for which $F$ is strictly obtuse and hence the only obtuse superbase, and, the lattice having finitely many obtuse superbases, the amount can be chosen so small that every conorm that is positive under $\Gmet$ for any of them stays positive under $\Gmet'$. Selling reduction under $\Gmet'$ started from $F'$ flips only at negative conorms. The first such conorm is zero under $\Gmet$, so the flip carries $F'$ to another obtuse superbase of $\Gmet$, and the same holds at every later step. Reduction terminates (Selling, 1874; Conway \& Sloane, 1992), at the only obtuse superbase of $\Gmet'$, which is $F$. So $F'$ is joined to $F$ by flips at zero conorms of $\Gmet$, and a swap matches the labellings. A symmetry maps $F_0$ to an obtuse superbase of the lattice; as a matrix it is that flip word followed by a swap.\hfill$\square$ Kurlin (2022) lists the superbases of each Voronoi type, and the enumeration of Lemma~A1 reproduces the counts.

\emph{Proposition A4 (the strain norm bounds every dot product).} If $\|\Gmet^{-1/2}\Delta\Gmet\,\Gmet^{-1/2}\|_F\le\epsilon$, then $|u^{\mathsf T}\Delta\Gmet\,v|\le\epsilon\,|u|\,|v|$ for all vectors $u,v$, lengths in $\Gmet$; in particular every conorm of every superbase changes by at most $\epsilon|u_i||u_j|$. \emph{Proof.} With $X=\Gmet^{-1/2}\Delta\Gmet\,\Gmet^{-1/2}$, $u^{\mathsf T}\Delta\Gmet\,v=(\Gmet^{1/2}u)^{\mathsf T}X(\Gmet^{1/2}v)$, at most $\|X\|_2|u||v|\le\|X\|_F|u||v|$.\hfill$\square$

\emph{Definition and enumeration A5 (sorts as closed sets of equalities).} Fix a zero pattern $Z$, let $O_Z$ be the arrows of its closure and, for $\Wop\in O_Z$, let $\mathbf A_\Wop$ be its linear action on conorm vectors (supporting information, Section~S3), which on the face $\{p\ge0,\ p_Z=0\}$ is a permutation of the slots. For a subspace $V$ of the face put $\mathrm{Sym}(V)=\{\Wop\in O_Z: \mathbf A_\Wop=\mathrm{id}\ \text{on}\ V\}$ and $\mathrm{Fix}(H)=\{p: \mathbf A_\Wop p=p\ \forall\,\Wop\in H\}$. A lattice with conorm vector $p$ on the face has point group $\mathrm{Sym}(\{p\})$, every symmetry being in $O_Z$ by Lemma~A3. We take as the definition of a sort a maximal set of lattices with the same kind of closure and the same symmetry operators; then the sorts of kind $Z$ are the generic points of the closed subspaces $V=\mathrm{Fix}(\mathrm{Sym}(V))$ that meet the interior of the face, modulo the arrows that map the face onto itself. This is the classical construction, the fixed subspace of a point group in the space of forms (Delone \emph{et al.}, 1975), written in conorm coordinates; SELLA uses the same subspaces. $\mathrm{Sym}$ and $\mathrm{Fix}$ are a Galois connection, so the closed subspaces are the intersections of the finitely many subspaces $\mathrm{Fix}(\Wop)$; since $\mathbf A_\Wop$ permutes the slots on the face, each is a set of equalities among conorms. Enumerating the intersections gives 8, 5, 3, 5 and 3 closed subspaces for no zero, one zero, two sharing a vector, two disjoint and three zeros, with symmetry orders $2,4,4,8,8,12,16,48$; $2,4,4,8,16$; $4,8,24$; $2,4,8,12,48$; $8,16,48$. A point of a closed subspace that lies in the interior of the face has exactly the symmetries $\mathrm{Sym}(V)$; points on its boundary belong to another zero pattern. Matched pattern by pattern, by computation, these are Delone's 24 sorts (Table~\ref{tab:sorts}).

\emph{Lemma A6 (the widened set is finite).} If $F$ is within strain $\epsilon<1$ of obtuse for $\Gmet$, every vector $u$ of $F$ satisfies $|u|^2_{\Gmet}\le\frac{1+\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}\,\mu_c(\Gmet)$, with $\mu_c$ the squared length of the shortest vector of the parity class of $u$, which is that of $F_0$'s partial sum in the class. \emph{Proof.} Let $\Gmet'$ within $\epsilon$ make $F$ obtuse. By Proposition~A4 with $u=v$, $(1-\epsilon)|u|^2_{\Gmet}\le|u|^2_{\Gmet'}\le(1+\epsilon)|u|^2_{\Gmet}$ for every lattice vector. The vectors of $F$ are shortest in their classes for $\Gmet'$, and those of $F_0$ for $\Gmet$, so $|u|^2_{\Gmet'}\le|v^0_c|^2_{\Gmet'}\le(1+\epsilon)\mu_c(\Gmet)$ and $|u|^2_{\Gmet}\le|u|^2_{\Gmet'}/(1-\epsilon)$.\hfill$\square$ The lattice vectors in these seven balls are finitely many and computable from $\Gmet$, so $\Se(\Gmet)$ is enumerated exactly.


\emph{Remark A7 (layers, and the lookup of $\mathcal U$).} Two superbases are \emph{adjacent} when some positive definite metric makes both obtuse; a superbase adjacent to $F_0$ is $\Wop F_0$ with $\Wop\in\mathcal U$, since both are superbases of that metric's closure. Let $\Lay_1=\mathcal U F_0$, $\Lay_k=\mathcal U\Lay_{k-1}$, and let $r_k(\Gmet)$ be the smallest strain from $\Gmet$ to a metric for which a superbase outside $\Lay_k$ is obtuse. If $\epsilon<r_k(\Gmet)$, then $\Se(\Gmet)$ is the set of members of $\Lay_k$ within strain $\epsilon$ of obtuse; this is the definition of $r_k$, and its content is the description of $\Lay_1$, which rests on the computer-assisted completeness of $\mathcal U$ (Lemma~A1). \emph{The lookup.} Let $\Pop$ be an operator of price at most $\epsilon$ from a reference with reduced basis $B_R$ to a new cell with reduced basis $B_N$, and let $e^\epsilon-1<r_1$ for the reference. By Lemma~A8 the new cell's reduced superbase, carried back by $\Pop$, lies in $\mathcal S_{e^\epsilon-1}(\Gmet_R)\subseteq\Lay_1$, so it is $\Wop F_0$ for some $\Wop\in\mathcal U$ in some labelling, and $\Pop=B_R\Wop\mathbf QB_N^{-1}$ with $\mathbf Q$ a swap. Since $\mathcal U$ is closed under swaps, $\Wop\mathbf Q\in\mathcal U$: trying $B_R\Wop B_N^{-1}$ for the 6096 matrices $\Wop$ finds $\Pop$.\hfill$\square$ The radii are computed exactly from the balls of Lemma~A6 at a strain of $\frac14$. No lower bound on $r_1$ in terms of the cell's geometry is proved; for the cells of Table~\ref{tab:radii} below the cap it is of the order of the ratio of the shortest to the longest edge.

\emph{Lemma A8 (widening one side suffices).} (i) If $\dist(\mathbf A,\mathbf B)\le\epsilon$, then $\|\mathbf B-\mathbf A\|_{\mathbf A}\le e^\epsilon-1$. (ii) Let $\Pop$ be an operator from a reference with metric $\Gmet_R$ to a new cell with metric $\Gmet_N$, of price at most $\epsilon$, and let $F$ be obtuse for $\Gmet_N$. Then $\Pop F\in\mathcal S_{e^\epsilon-1}(\Gmet_R)$. \emph{Proof.} (i) The eigenvalues of $\mathbf A^{-1/2}\mathbf B\mathbf A^{-1/2}$ are $e^{l_i}$ with $\sum l_i^2\le\epsilon^2$, and $|e^{l}-1|\le|l|(e^{a}-1)/a$ for $|l|\le a$, because $(e^x-1)/x$ increases; with $a=\max|l_i|\le\epsilon$, $\|\mathbf B-\mathbf A\|_{\mathbf A}=(\sum(e^{l_i}-1)^2)^{1/2}\le\epsilon\,(e^\epsilon-1)/\epsilon$. (ii) The price is $\dist(\Gmet_N,s\Pop^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_R\Pop)$ for the fitted scale $s$. Carried back by $\Pop$, an isometry of both measures, $\Pop F$ is obtuse for $\mathbf M=\Pop^{-\mathsf T}\Gmet_N\Pop^{-1}$, and $\dist(s\Gmet_R,\mathbf M)\le\epsilon$, so by (i) $\mathbf M$ is within strain $e^\epsilon-1$ of $s\Gmet_R$; the strain and the widened set are unchanged by the scale.\hfill$\square$ For the price band of $7.4\%$ the reference is widened at $7.7\%$.

\emph{Proposition A9 (the price of a symmetry).} Let $g$ be an integer matrix of finite order $n$ and $V_g=\{\Gmet:g^{\mathsf T}\Gmet g=\Gmet\}$ the metrics for which $g$ is a symmetry. (i) $\dist(\Gmet,g^{\mathsf T}\Gmet g)\le2\dist(\Gmet,V_g)$, with equality when $g$ is a twofold. (ii) If $g$ is a twofold along a lattice row of obliquity $\delta$ in $\Gmet$ (the angle between the row and the normal to the lattice plane that $g$ negates), then $\dist(\Gmet,g^{\mathsf T}\Gmet g)=2\sqrt2\,\mathrm{artanh}(\sin\delta)$. (iii) For a metric $\Gmet^*\in V_g$ and the strain at $\Gmet^*$, $\|g^{\mathsf T}\Gmet g-\Gmet\|_{\Gmet^*}$ lies between $\rho_{\min}$ and $\rho_{\max}$ times the distance from $\Gmet$ to $V_g$ in that norm, with $\rho=|e^{i\theta}-1|$ over the rotation angles $\theta\ne0$ of $X\mapsto g^{\mathsf T}Xg$: $\rho=2$ for a twofold, $\sqrt3$ for a threefold, $\{\sqrt2,2\}$ for a fourfold and $\{1,\sqrt3\}$ for a sixfold. The scale fit leaves these prices unchanged, since $\det g^{\mathsf T}\Gmet g=\det\Gmet$. \emph{Proof.} (i) $\Gmet\mapsto g^{\mathsf T}\Gmet g$ is an isometry of $\dist$. For any $\Gmet'\in V_g$, $\dist(\Gmet,g^{\mathsf T}\Gmet g)\le\dist(\Gmet,\Gmet')+\dist(\Gmet',g^{\mathsf T}\Gmet g)=2\dist(\Gmet,\Gmet')$. For a twofold, the geodesic midpoint $m$ of $\Gmet$ and $g^{\mathsf T}\Gmet g$ is unique and $g$ exchanges the two ends, so $m\in V_g$ and $\dist(\Gmet,V_g)\le\frac12\dist(\Gmet,g^{\mathsf T}\Gmet g)$. (ii) In Cartesian coordinates for $\Gmet$, $g$ becomes $T=\Gmet^{1/2}g\Gmet^{-1/2}$, an involution fixing the line $\ell$ of the row and negating the plane $\Pi$; it is $2\mathbf P-I$ with $\mathbf P$ the projection onto $\ell$ along $\Pi$, and the angle between $\ell$ and the normal of $\Pi$ is $\delta$. The price is $\|\ln(T^{\mathsf T}T)\|_F$. $T^{\mathsf T}T$ is the identity on the line of $\Pi$ normal to $\ell$, and on the plane spanned by $\ell$ and the normal of $\Pi$ it has eigenvalues $(1\pm\sin\delta)/(1\mp\sin\delta)$, whose logarithms are $\pm2\,\mathrm{artanh}(\sin\delta)$. (iii) $\sigma:X\mapsto g^{\mathsf T}Xg$ is an isometry of the inner product $\mathrm{tr}(\Gmet^{*-1}X\Gmet^{*-1}Y)$ of order $n$, so the space of symmetric matrices splits orthogonally into its fixed space, which is $V_g-\Gmet^*$, and planes and lines on which it rotates by $\theta=2\pi k/n$. With $\Gmet-\Gmet^*=X_0+X_\perp$, $X_0$ fixed, the distance to $V_g$ is $\|X_\perp\|$ and $g^{\mathsf T}\Gmet g-\Gmet=(\sigma-1)X_\perp$. The angles are the pairwise products of the eigenvalues of $g$.\hfill$\square$ The metrics of a sort to which $g$ belongs lie inside $V_g$ (read in the frame in which the sort's distance is attained), so, to first order in the error, where the strain of a sort distance and the geodesic distance agree, a twofold's price is at most twice the distance to that sort, with equality when $V_g$ is the sort's subspace, as for the twofold of a primitive monoclinic lattice.

\emph{Proposition A10 (candidates and cosets).} Let $\Pop_0$ be the true operator from a reference $R$ to a new cell $N$, of price $p_0$; let $H_\epsilon$ be the proper unimodular $h$ with $\pi(h)=\dist(\Gmet_R,h^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_Rh)\le\epsilon$, a finite set; and let $C_\epsilon$ be the candidates, the proper unimodular operators of price at most $\epsilon$. Then (i) $\pi(h_1h_2)\le\pi(h_1)+\pi(h_2)$; (ii) $H_{\epsilon-p_0}\Pop_0\subseteq C_\epsilon\subseteq H_{\epsilon+p_0}\Pop_0$; (iii) if no self-map of $R$ has price in $(\epsilon,2\epsilon]$, $H_\epsilon$ is a group; (iv) if none has price in $(\epsilon-p_0,\epsilon+p_0]$, then $C_\epsilon=H_\epsilon\Pop_0$. \emph{Proof.} The price is the geodesic distance between metrics normalised to unit determinant, and unimodular matrices act on those by isometries. (i) $\dist(\Gmet_R,(h_1h_2)^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_Rh_1h_2)\le\dist(\Gmet_R,h_2^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_Rh_2)+\dist(h_2^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_Rh_2,h_2^{\mathsf T}h_1^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_Rh_1h_2)=\pi(h_2)+\pi(h_1)$. (ii) By the triangle inequality the price of $h\Pop_0$ differs from $\dist(\Pop_0^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_R\Pop_0,\Pop_0^{\mathsf T}h^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_Rh\Pop_0)=\pi(h)$ by at most $p_0$, and every unimodular $\Pop$ is $h\Pop_0$ with $h=\Pop\Pop_0^{-1}$. (iii) By (i) a product of two elements of $H_\epsilon$ has price at most $2\epsilon$, hence at most $\epsilon$; a finite set of matrices closed under products is a group. (iv) The two sets of (ii) then coincide.\hfill$\square$ The conditions are sufficient, not necessary: the worked cell at $\epsilon=7.4\%$ has self-maps of price $0$, $1.55\%$ and $10.2\%$, so the condition of (iii) fails, yet $H_\epsilon=\{\mathbf I,2_y\}$ is a group, which the implementation checks directly.

\section{Distances to the 24 sorts}
\label{app:spectrum}
%==============================================================================

The same computation gives the distance from a measured cell to each of the 24 sorts. In each of the 6096 frames of $\mathcal U$ the cell's conorms are projected, in the strain, onto the sort's subspace (its zeros and equalities, Definition~A5, the other conorms non-negative), and the smallest distance is taken. This is the computation of SELLA (Andrews \emph{et al.}, 2023), which projects the boundary images of a cell in $S^6$ onto the same subspaces, and of Oishi-Tomiyasu (2012) in Selling coordinates; projection onto Bravais-lattice subspaces goes back to Andrews \& Bernstein (1988). What changes is the norm, which is the same in every frame, and the set of frames, which is $\mathcal U$. We call the 24 distances the \emph{sort spectrum} of the cell. A distance below the adjacency radius $r_1$ is exact, since the frames of $\mathcal U$ then contain the frame of the nearest lattice of the sort; a minimum above $r_1$ is an upper bound over the frames used, the true distance being at least $r_1$, and the implementation flags it (\texttt{certified\_sort\_spectrum}). Along every specialisation of Table~S2 the distance cannot decrease, since the lower sort's subspace lies inside the upper one's.

Fig.~\ref{fig:sortgraph} draws the spectrum of the worked cell on the graph of specialisations. The nearest sorts are A2 at $0.28\%$, M4 (mP) at $0.78\%$, A3 at $3.8\%$ and O5 (oP) at $5.1\%$, and every other sort is at least $25\%$ away, a bound that holds because $r_1$ reaches the cap for this cell. For a tetragonal lysozyme cell indexed in $P1$ the twelve sorts from aP to tP all lie within $0.22\%$ (supporting information, Fig.~S1).

\emph{Le Page's $3^\circ$ by sort.} The distance to a sort at Le Page's boundary depends on the sort, while the price of each operator does not. For exact lattices of each sort perturbed in random directions until Le Page's obliquity for the sort's Bravais group is exactly $3^\circ$, the median largest twofold price is $14.8\%$ in every sort, as Proposition~A9 requires (larger only in the monoclinic tails, where Le Page's group is another C-centred group), while the median distance to the sort is $7.4$--$7.7\%$ for the orthorhombic and monoclinic sorts other than O3, M2B and M3, $7.9$--$8.1\%$ for tP, tI, hP and hR, and $8.3$--$8.5\%$ for the cubic sorts, with longer tails for the boundary sorts O3, M2B and M3 (Table~\ref{tab:lepage}). A single band on the distance to a sort therefore tests each sort at a different level, which is why the reindexing in this paper is decided on the prices of operators.

\emph{Against BLDConograph and SELLA.} On the seven sample cells that Oishi-Tomiyasu (2024) distributes with BLDConograph, the highest Bravais type within $7.4\%$ is the program's type in all seven, with the next type at least $14.9\%$ away (supporting information, Table~S5). SELLA was run on the worked cell, the lysozyme cell, a pseudo-tetragonal cell, a cell with all angles $92^\circ$, a needle, two plates and the seven BLDConograph cells. The two rankings of the 24 sorts agree closely, with Spearman's rank correlation $0.77$ to $0.97$. The two scales are not proportional: SELLA's distance divided by the cell's mean squared edge is a median $0.71$ times the strain distance, with a wide spread between cells. With SELLA's band set by that median ratio, the Bravais types within the band agree in 11 of the 14 cells. They differ on the needle and the two plates, where an absolute band weighs the long edges differently: SELLA admits hP, hR, oF, tI and tP for the needle, which are far in strain, and the strain band admits oF (and tI) for the plates, which SELLA's does not. For these thin cells $r_1$ is $4$ to $6\%$, so their strain distances above it are bounds and are flagged.
