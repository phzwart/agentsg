\begin{synopsis}
The complete, finite set of obtuse superbases of a lattice --- its Selling closure --- supports symmetry assignment, reindexing, deformation comparison and archive search, each with a stated guarantee under one declared noise scale, and replaces angular symmetry gates and fixed lists of small matrices.
\end{synopsis}

\begin{abstract}
A unit cell is one basis of a lattice, and a lattice has infinitely many. Crystallographic software performs four operations on such families of bases --- assigning lattice symmetry, reindexing one cell onto another, comparing lattices and searching archives --- and classically each rests on a tolerance or a fixed list for which no completeness statement exists: an angular symmetry gate, a fixed set of small integer matrices, a reduced cell that jumps at reduction boundaries. All four operations follow from one finite object. The Selling closure of a lattice is the complete set of its obtuse superbases, together with the integer changes of basis between them; it is enumerated by a walk over Selling moves at vanishing conorms, and its completeness is a theorem (Kurlin, 2026). Its members are related by integer matrices with entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$, proved for every lattice type, and widened to a declared noise scale it is stable under measurement error. Matching two closures gives the distance between lattices --- the smallest metric perturbation that makes them congruent --- with a termination certificate and the full coset of relating operators. Matching a closure with itself gives the exact automorphism group and, at the declared noise, the pseudo-symmetry operators, each with a price in \Ang$^2$: twice the smallest perturbation that would make it exact. Every Bravais class is a list of linear constraints on the conorms, so the nexus points of the Bravais skeleton follow by solving stacked lists. The $3^\circ$ angular gate is superseded, and so is the fixed list of 6\,960 matrices, which runs without a certificate on seven of seventeen benchmark cells. Sorting the six conorms collapses the closure to a Euclidean key whose distance is a lower bound on the aligned distance, exact on one-chamber serial families. Applied to 206\,214 PDB cells the key gives an atlas organised by the computed skeleton; applied to 1\,065 carbonic anhydrase entries the closure certifies every pairwise distance; applied to a 12\,474-crystal XFEL stream the key is exact. Every guarantee is stated under one declared noise scale, and the lattices for which no metric method can decide are flagged before output.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================

A lattice is a set of points. A unit cell describes that set by three vectors $v_1,v_2,v_3$, and the metric tensor $\Gmet=\Bbasis^{\mathsf T}\Bbasis$ collects their lengths and angles. Any integer matrix $\Mop$ of determinant $\pm1$ produces another equally valid cell for the same lattice, $\Bbasis'=\Bbasis\Mop$ and $\Gmet'=\Mop^{\mathsf T}\Gmet\Mop$, so a lattice is not one metric tensor but an infinite family of them. The traditional way to remove the ambiguity is reduction, a rule that selects one cell from the family (Niggli, 1928; Grosse-Kunstleve \emph{et al.}, 2004). For reporting a cell in a conventional form it is exactly right, but a rule that picks one representative must decide what to do where two representatives are equally good, and there the chosen cell changes abruptly while the lattice changes smoothly.\chk{z:trajectory}

Crystallographic software performs four operations on these families. It assigns lattice symmetry, usually by scanning for near-twofold axes behind an angular gate (Le Page, 1982). It reindexes one cell onto another, usually by testing a fixed list of small integer matrices or by reading operators off the gated point group. It compares lattices, usually through reduced-cell parameters. And it searches archives of hundreds of thousands of cells, and streams of thousands of serial-crystallography frames. Each operation rests on a tolerance or a list for which no completeness statement exists: the angular gate admits and misses operators in a unit that is not the data's, the matrix list is complete only for cells of bounded anisotropy, and a reduced-cell comparison inherits the jumps of its representative.

This paper shows that all four operations follow from one finite object. The \emph{Selling closure} of a lattice --- the complete set of its obtuse superbases, with the integer changes of basis between them --- is finite, enumerable and complete as a set. Matching two closures gives the distance between lattices with a termination certificate and the full coset of relating operators; matching a closure with itself gives the lattice's exact symmetry and, at declared noise, its pseudo-symmetries, each priced in the data's own unit; reindexing reads its operators off the matched members; and sorting the closure's six conorms collapses it to a Euclidean search key. Measured cells enter through one declared noise scale, and the lattices for which no metric method can decide are identified before output.

The ingredients are classical or recent, and the paper is explicit about which is which. Selling reduction and the obtuse superbase go back to Selling, Delone and Bravais (Bravais, 1850; Delone, 1932); the conorm and vonorm language is that of Conway \& Sloane (1992); the crystallographic $S^6$ embedding, the uniqueness of the reduced Selling scalars as a list and the practical reduction algorithm are due to Andrews, Bernstein and Sauter (Andrews \& Bernstein, 1988, 2014, 2016, 2023; Andrews \emph{et al.}, 2019); and the type-dependent enumeration of obtuse superbases, the completeness of the resulting isometry classification and the perturbation bounds on root products are Kurlin's (Bright \emph{et al.}, 2021; Kurlin, 2026). What is added here is the closure as a working object: its widening to measured data, the matching that returns distances and cosets, the entry bound of its operators, the pricing of symmetry, and the sorted key with its lower-bound lemma.

Section~\ref{sec:closure-def} defines the closure and follows one cell through it. Section~\ref{sec:apps} derives five applications from it, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports results in the same order. Proofs are in Appendices~A--E, and every statement that can be checked by computation carries a mark $^{[\mathrm{S}n]}$ pointing to the check in the Supporting Information. One notation is fixed throughout: $\eta$ is the declared noise scale on the metric tensor (Frobenius norm, \Ang$^2$), and $\tau=3\eta$ is the widening it implies.

\section{The Selling closure}
\label{sec:closure-def}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Conorms and the obtuse cone}
\label{sec:conorms}

Add a fourth vector $v_0=-(v_1+v_2+v_3)$ to a primitive basis: the four vectors sum to zero and form a \emph{superbase}. Its six \emph{conorms} $p_{ij}=-v_i\cdot v_j$, $0\le i<j\le3$, are the negatives of the Selling scalars of Andrews \emph{et al.} (2019), and the metric tensor is linear in them:
\begin{equation}
\Gmet=\begin{pmatrix}p_{01}+p_{12}+p_{13}&-p_{12}&-p_{13}\\-p_{12}&p_{02}+p_{12}+p_{23}&-p_{23}\\-p_{13}&-p_{23}&p_{03}+p_{13}+p_{23}\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:gram}
\end{equation}
A perturbation of the metric therefore moves the conorms by the same amount, in the same unit, and every tolerance below inherits that unit. A superbase is \emph{obtuse} --- Selling reduced --- when all six conorms are non-negative. Reduced lattices fill the non-negative orthant of conorm space, the obtuse cone, whose faces $p_{ij}=0$ are the \emph{walls} of reduction; a wall is a right angle somewhere in the superbase. A negative conorm is removed by a Selling move, which lowers the conorm sum, and repeated moves terminate (Appendix~A).\chk{z:reduction-step}

Two operations never change the lattice, and are discarded throughout. Reordering the four vectors is an element of $S_4$, the symmetric group on four letters (24 elements): the zero-sum condition is symmetric in the four vectors, so none of them is special, and a reordering merely permutes the six conorm slots. Negating all four vectors leaves every conorm unchanged. Together they give 48 labellings of one geometric superbase. In the $S^6$ formulation the same coordinates appear with opposite sign, the reduced region is the non-positive orthant, and the Selling moves are the boundary transformations at its faces (Appendix~A).

\subsection{The closure}
\label{sec:closure}

For a generic lattice the obtuse superbase is unique up to the 48 labellings. For a symmetric lattice it is not: further obtuse superbases arise from the reduced one by Selling moves at conorms that are exactly zero (Kurlin, 2026). The \emph{Selling closure} $\Sclosure(\Lambda)$ is the complete set of obtuse superbases of the lattice $\Lambda$, each stored with its integer coordinates in the input basis. Kurlin's lemmas supply the two facts everything below stands on. The closure is \emph{complete}: no obtuse superbase exists outside it. And it is \emph{enumerable}: start from any member and apply the move at every vanishing conorm until nothing new appears. For an exact lattice no search, tie-break or tolerance enters. Table~\ref{tab:closure} gives the size of the closure by Voronoi type, and the type is read off the zero pattern of the reduced conorms.\chk{z:closure-count}\chk{z:type-rule} The classical statement that the obtuse superbases of a lattice are ``24 relabellings'' of one holds only for lattices without symmetry; monoclinic P lattices are V4 and orthorhombic P lattices V5,\chk{z:pdb-types} so for much of the PDB it undercounts.

The members of a closure are close to one another in a precise sense. On each Voronoi stratum the closure, written in the integer coordinates of one member, is a fixed finite set --- the same integers for every lattice of the type --- and every coordinate has entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$. This is a machine-checked proposition over the five stratum-fixed flip graphs (Appendix~A).\chk{z:brute-complete} The integers depend only on which conorms vanish, never on the cell parameters.

\subsection{The change-of-basis web}
\label{sec:web}

Any two members $i$ and $j$ are related by an integer matrix of determinant $\pm1$, the change of basis $\Wop_{ij}$ from one to the other, and these matrices are consistent: composing along any path through the members gives the same matrix, $\Wop_{ij}\Wop_{jk}=\Wop_{ik}$, and $\Wop_{ji}=\Wop_{ij}^{-1}$. The automorphisms of the lattice are exactly the web operators that leave the metric invariant, and each permutes the members. Any operator $\Pop$ that aligns two closures does the same: it induces a bijection $\sigma$ of members and carries the whole web along, $\Wop'_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}=\Pop\,\Wop_{ij}\,\Pop^{-1}$.\chk{z:transport} The web is the object the applications use: every operator relating two settings, two lattices, or a lattice to itself is read off it.

\subsection{Measured cells: the widened closure}
\label{sec:widening}

The exact closure is discontinuous in the data. A conorm that is zero in the true lattice is a small positive or negative number in a measurement, and every member whose conorms dip below zero is discarded. The repair is to move the obtuse boundary. The \emph{widened closure} $\Sclosure_\tau(\Lambda)$ is the set of superbases whose six conorms all exceed $-\tau$, with $\tau=3\eta$; it is enumerated by the same walk, allowed to cross any wall nearer than $\tau$. It is canonical, because it is defined by a membership property and so does not depend on where the walk starts; it is complete, because the walk run with a band of $2\tau$ provably reaches every qualifying superbase; and it is finite, because the conorm bound caps the vector lengths. What it costs is the uniqueness of a single reduced representative: inside the band, reduction order decides which nearly obtuse frame one stops at, which is why the object compared is always the set. Two propositions keep the widening inside Kurlin's theorem (Appendix~B). \emph{Overlap}: if two metrics differ by at most $\eta$, every member of either lattice's exact closure is a member of the other's widened closure, at the same integer coordinates. \emph{Clipping}: every widened member, with its negative conorms set to zero, is an exact obtuse superbase of an explicitly constructed lattice within $2\sqrt6\,\tau$, so completeness is never invoked off the obtuse cone.\chk{z:overlap}

\subsection{One cell, start to finish}
\label{sec:thread}

Take the monoclinic cell $30$--$40$--$50$\,\Ang, $\beta=92^\circ$. Its natural superbase has conorms $(847.7, 1600.0, 2447.7, 0, 52.3, 0)$\,\Ang$^2$: the two zeros are the two right angles, so the cell sits on two walls. The walk visits 12 labelled obtuse superbases, six after discarding labellings (Table~\ref{tab:example}). Every coordinate is $0$ or $\pm1$, and every member carries the same six conorms in a different order, hence the same sorted key (Section~\ref{sec:key}).\chk{z:transport} Now nudge every parameter --- $30.02$, $39.97$, $50.03$\,\Ang, $90.25^\circ$, $92.05^\circ$, $89.80^\circ$ --- so that the metric moves by $\eta=14.4$\,\Ang$^2$. The two zeros become $\pm4.2$ and $\pm4.5$\,\Ang$^2$, five of the six members acquire a negative slot, and the exact closure of the nudged cell has one member. Its widened closure at $\tau=3\eta=43.2$\,\Ang$^2$, computed from the nudged cell alone, has sixteen members, and all six original members are among them at the same integer coordinates.\chk{z:transport} The same pair returns in every application below: matched, it gives its distance and coset (Section~\ref{sec:match}); matched with itself, its symmetry and the price of more (Section~\ref{sec:symmetry}).

\section{Applications of the closure}
\label{sec:apps}
%==============================================================================

Each application states the task, the classical route and its gap, the closure route, and the guarantee. All of them use the matching of Section~\ref{sec:match}.

\subsection{Matching two closures: distance and certificate}
\label{sec:match}
\label{sec:ideal}

The distance between lattices $A$ and $B$ is defined here as the smallest perturbation of $A$'s metric that makes it congruent to $B$. Congruence is an integer change of basis, so the perturbations that achieve it form the discrete set $\Delta\Gmet=\Mop^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_B\Mop-\Gmet_A$, one per unimodular $\Mop$, and the smallest is
\begin{equation}
d(A\!\to\!B)=\min_{\Mop}\ \big\|\,\Gmet_A-\Mop^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_B\Mop\,\big\|,
\label{eq:ideal}
\end{equation}
in the Frobenius norm, reported symmetrised as $d(A,B)=\max\{d(A\!\to\!B),\,d(B\!\to\!A)\}$. The minimum is attained and is zero exactly at congruence, and its minimiser converts directly into the parameter changes that deform $A$ into a copy of $B$ (Appendix~C).\chk{z:deformation} As written it ranges over an infinite group; the closure makes it finite.

If $A$ and $B$ are congruent, their closures are one geometric object in two coordinate systems, so matching members attains the minimum, zero, and returns the full relating coset.\chk{z:brute-complete} If they are not, the minimiser need not present $B$ as an obtuse frame, but it nearly does: a frame achieving value $D$ penetrates the obtuse cone by at most $\sqrt3\,D$ (observed worst case $0.70\,D$).\chk{z:deformation} Matching against the widened closure at $\sqrt3\,D_0$, with $D_0$ the plain-closure minimum, therefore reaches the ideal, and the search stops with a certificate once the widening covers the value found. One case closes at once, the \emph{margin certificate}: if $D_0$ is smaller than every positive conorm of both lattices, no frame that near either lattice can cross a wall, and $D_0$ is the ideal.\chk{z:closure-metric}

Matching proceeds in three steps. \emph{Propose}: each pair of members, $i$ from $\Sclosure(A)$ and $j$ from $\Sclosure(B)$ under each labelling, proposes one operator $\Pop=\Cmat_j\Cmat_i^{-1}$ from their integer coordinates, integer and unimodular by construction. \emph{Verify}: $\Pop$ is accepted when its residual $\|\Pop^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_B\Pop-\Gmet_A\|$ lies within the band set by $\eta$. \emph{Transport}: an accepted $\Pop$ aligns the whole set, permuting members and conjugating the web of Section~\ref{sec:web}, so a candidate that pairs members without pairing their relations is rejected without further machinery.\chk{z:transport}

The residuals of all candidates are themselves the diagnosis. On the pair of Section~\ref{sec:thread}, the $6\times16$ proposals fall into three levels: twelve at $14.4$\,\Ang$^2$ --- the distance, equal to $\eta$, from four distinct operators forming the $2/m$ coset; twelve at $150.7$\,\Ang$^2$, the pseudo-$mmm$ operators, proposed only by pairings into the widened members; and nothing below $992$\,\Ang$^2$ after that.\chk{z:transport} The first level is the distance, the second is the pseudo-symmetry margin of Section~\ref{sec:symmetry}, and the gap between them certifies that the alignment is unique. Restricted to the plain closure, the match is a congruence test rather than a metric: between genuinely different lattices it is discontinuous across walls (Appendix~C), and the widened match or the key is the right object for similarity.

\subsection{Symmetry assignment and its price}
\label{sec:symmetry}
\label{sec:pseudo}

The classical route to lattice symmetry scans for lattice directions that are twofold axes to within an angular tolerance and assembles the point group from them (Le Page, 1982). The closure route asks which integer operators carry the metric onto itself, and matching the closure with itself answers it exactly: the accepted set is the automorphism group $H=\mathrm{Aut}(\Lambda)$, complete because the closure is, with no tolerance for an exact lattice.\chk{z:coset}

\emph{Classes are constraint lists.} In conorm language every Bravais class is a pattern of zeros and equalities: a zero conorm is a wall, an equality a tie (Delone, 1932; Conway \& Sloane, 1992). Table~\ref{tab:patterns} gives the pattern for representative cells: two zeros for monoclinic P, three for orthorhombic P, three zeros and one tie for tetragonal P, two zeros and a triple tie for hexagonal P, and six equal conorms with no zero at all for body-centred cubic.\chk{z:sym-profile} Each pattern is a short list of linear equations on the conorms, imposed on whole relabelling orbits, and therefore --- by equation~(\ref{eq:gram}) --- a list of linear equations on the metric. Nearness to a class is nearness to the solution set of its list, which is what makes symmetry quantitative. It also makes the meeting points of classes computable: two classes meet where their stacked lists have a common solution. A common solution with a positive-definite metric is a \emph{nexus}; a common solution only on the singular boundary of the cone, at infinite axial ratio, is a \emph{seam}, where two families converge without meeting (Section~\ref{sec:pdb}).\chk{z:nexus}

\emph{Near symmetry is a coset.} A measured lattice is never exactly on a pattern, so the question becomes which operators are symmetries to within the noise. These are the $\eta$-automorphisms $\mathrm{Aut}_\eta(\Lambda)=\{Q:\|Q^{\mathsf T}\Gmet Q-\Gmet\|\le\eta\}$, returned by the same self-match with the verification band loosened. One proposition fixes their structure (Appendix~C): the near-optimal alignments of two closures form a single coset $\Pop_0\,\mathrm{Aut}_{2\eta}(B)$, so ambiguity is never amorphous, and the alignment is unique exactly when $2\eta$ lies below the \emph{pseudo-symmetry margin} $s(B)$, the first nonzero value of $B$'s self-spectrum. On a monoclinic cell with $\beta=90^\circ+\delta$ the coset is the four operators of $2/m$ below the margin and the eight of pseudo-$mmm$ above it, the margin scales linearly with $\delta$ (296 and $29.6$\,\Ang$^2$ at $\delta=2^\circ$ and $0.2^\circ$), and the returned set is group-closed --- the certificate that no coset was cut in half.\chk{z:align-coset}

\emph{Every rung has a price.} Imposing a symmetry means solving its constraint list for the smallest change of the metric, a closed-form least-squares problem because the list is linear: for one wall slot, empty it and pour its value into its two diagonal partners, at cost $\sqrt2\,p$. For an operator of order two, $Q^{\mathsf T}\Gmet Q$ is the reflection of $\Gmet$ through the metrics that $Q$ fixes, so its residual is exactly twice the distance to them: every rung of the self-spectrum sits at twice the minimum cost of the symmetry it represents (Appendix~C). On the parent cell of Section~\ref{sec:thread}, zeroing the one off-wall conorm costs $\sqrt2\times52.35=74.0$\,\Ang$^2$, half of $s(B)=148.1$\,\Ang$^2$, and lands exactly on $30$--$40$--$50$, $90^\circ\,90^\circ\,90^\circ$, whose eight $mmm$ operators are then exact and whose closure grows from 6 to 16 members. On the nudged cell the self-spectrum reads 0 (triclinic), $27.4=2\times13.7$\,\Ang$^2$ (the monoclinic wall stratum, whose operators are the setting changes a reduction can jump between) and $152$--$154=2\times77.2$\,\Ang$^2$ ($mmm$): one ladder covers reduction jumps and pseudo-symmetry, each at its price.\chk{z:sym-profile}

For twin-law and indexing analysis the nearby symmetric lattice $R^*$ is built by that constrained solve, its closure self-matched to $H^*=\mathrm{Aut}(R^*)\supseteq H$, and the pseudo-symmetry branches handed to intensity statistics are the cosets $H^*/L$, $L$ the Laue group, each extra operator carrying its residual.\chk{z:pseudo} Table~\ref{tab:ambiguity} separates the four sources of indexing ambiguity and where each is resolved.

\emph{Relation to the Le~Page search.} The twofold search approximates the quantity computed here: its angular obliquity is an anisotropic proxy for the residual $\|Q^{\mathsf T}\Gmet Q-\Gmet\|$, and the same $3^\circ$ corresponds to a different number of \Ang$^2$ on every cell. The closure route returns the same integer operators, each with its residual and its price in the data's unit and in group-closed cosets, and in addition the operators that relate two settings, which a near-symmetry gate drops once the lattice moves off the symmetric locus (Section~\ref{sec:res-sym}).\chk{z:lepage} The $3^\circ$ gate is superseded rather than refined.

\subsection{Reindexing}
\label{sec:reindex-proc}
\label{sec:exact}

Reindexing transforms indices, coordinates and symmetry operators from one setting to another and needs the relating operator exactly. The closure route reads it off the matched members:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Go primitive, and keep the way back.} Convert centred settings with the rational centering matrix $\Cmat$, $\Bbasis_{\rm prim}=\Bbasis_{\rm conv}\Cmat$. This is the only rational step.
\item \emph{Enumerate.} Reduce, and enumerate the (widened) closure with integer coordinates. For a reference, the closure, its web and $H=\mathrm{Aut}(R)$ are computed once and cached; at most 32 members.
\item \emph{Screen} (optional). Discard a frame whose sorted key lies outside the noise-derived radius (Section~\ref{sec:key}).
\item \emph{Match} (Section~\ref{sec:match}). Every member pair proposes $\Pop$; accept within the band.
\item \emph{Report the coset}, $\Pop_0H$, split by determinant sign if handedness matters. With a known space group the distinct indexing alternatives are the $|H|/|L|$ cosets of the Laue group, one representative each (Lebedev \emph{et al.}, 2006; Brehm \& Diederichs, 2014; Gildea \& Winter, 2018).\chk{z:coset-reps}
\item \emph{Propagate.} With $\Bbasis_N=\Bbasis_R\Pop$: $x_R=\Pop\,x_N$, $h_R=\Pop^{-\mathsf T}h_N$, $W_R=\Pop W_N\Pop^{-1}$; in conventional settings $\Pop_{\rm conv}=\Cmat_R\,\Pop\,\Cmat_N^{-1}$.
\end{enumerate}
Nothing is rounded: the operators are integer because they are built from integer coordinates, and floating point enters only at verification.\chk{z:reindex-proc} A new frame costs one reduction, one closure enumeration --- usually a single member --- and at most $32\times|\Sclosure(N)|\times48$ integer products.\chk{z:reindex-bench} With the retrieval radius, the zero tolerance and the verification band set at stated multiples of $\eta$, the measured pair is retrieved, the widened closure contains every true member, the full coset is accepted, and nothing false is (Appendix~B).\chk{z:guarantee} What no procedure decides is whether two cells are congruent within $\eta$ or distinct by less; that boundary is the noise model itself, and the decision belongs to intensity statistics.

\emph{Relation to fixed matrix lists.} The familiar list of 6\,960 unimodular matrices with entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$ is a truncation of the search in equation~(\ref{eq:ideal}), and its completeness has a precise condition. If a candidate achieves value $D$, comparing the two quadratic forms bounds the operator,
\begin{equation}
\|\Mop\|_2^{\,2}\;\le\;\frac{\lambda_{\max}(\Gmet_A)+D}{\lambda_{\min}(\Gmet_B)}\;=:\;R(D)^2,
\label{eq:cert}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\max}$ and $\lambda_{\min}$ the extreme eigenvalues of the two metrics. An entry of two needs $R\ge2$, so the list is certified exactly when $\lambda_{\max}(\Gmet_A)+D<4\lambda_{\min}(\Gmet_B)$ --- a condition each pair verifies, and one that seven of the seventeen benchmark cells of McCoy \emph{et al.} (2026) fail at zero error (Section~\ref{sec:declared}).\chk{z:declared-error} Between settings of one lattice the list always contains \emph{a} relating operator, by the proposition of Section~\ref{sec:closure}, but it provably misses parts of the coset: most reduced bases of V4 and V5 lattices carry self-coset operators with an entry of two, and cuboid settings from different arrangement classes are related by an entry of three.\chk{z:brute-complete} The closure route needs no entry bound: whatever large integers the input bases carry are absorbed by the reduction bookkeeping and reproduced in the operator.

\subsection{Comparison under deformation}
\label{sec:deform}

Between different lattices the match of Section~\ref{sec:match} is a deformation distance, and its minimiser is the deformation: $\Delta\Gmet$ converts into parameter changes, and into a strain against a reference. The certificate decides whether the value reported is the ideal --- under the margin certificate the plain closure minimum is exact, and otherwise the widened enumeration stops once the widening covers the value. On the Hfq pair of deposited cells (4nl7, 2yht) the two directed values, 492 and 533\,\Ang$^2$, are certified global minima; their difference is the asymmetry of anchoring, which the symmetrised report absorbs.\chk{z:deformation} For benchmarking the error is declared rather than assumed: a reduced metric is perturbed by $\Delta\Gmet=D\,E$ with $E$ a random symmetric matrix of unit Frobenius norm, so the error radius $D$, the widening $\sqrt3\,D$ and the certificate radius $R(D)$ are all in \Ang$^2$ and directly comparable (Section~\ref{sec:declared}).\chk{z:declared-error}

\subsection{The linear key}
\label{sec:key}

Search cannot afford a match per pair. Every member of the closure carries the same six conorms in some order (Table~\ref{tab:example}), so discarding the order discards nothing that distinguishes members. The archive key divides the conorms by the square root of their sum and sorts them:
\begin{equation}
\sortkey(\Lambda)=\sort\big(p_{ij}/\sqrt{T}\big),\qquad T=\sum_{i<j}p_{ij} .
\label{eq:key}
\end{equation}
$T$ is unchanged by a move at a zero conorm, so every basis of a lattice yields the same key.\chk{z:one-key} The key is linear in the conorms, hence Lipschitz in the metric, and it scales as a length; a cube of edge $a$ has key $(0,0,0,a/\sqrt3,a/\sqrt3,a/\sqrt3)$. Sorting forgets which conorm belonged to which pair, and that is a lower bound:

\begin{lemma}[conservative sorted-key distance]
\label{lem:lb}
Let $x,y\in\mathbb R^6$ and let $G\subseteq S_6$ be any set of permutations. Then
\begin{equation}
\|\sort(x)-\sort(y)\|_2=\min_{\sigma\in S_6}\|x-\sigma y\|_2\le\min_{\sigma\in G}\|x-\sigma y\|_2 .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

With $G$ the image of $S_4$, the key distance is a lower bound on the aligned distance over the two closures, so a radius query returns every lattice within the radius, plus occasionally impostors, which matching removes.\chk{z:lowerbound} The key lives on a flat convex cone, the quotient of $\mathbb R^6$ by all permutations, so KD trees and principal components apply to it unchanged; the quotient by the smaller exact relabelling group, where Kurlin's complete invariant lives, is not Euclidean (Appendix~D).\chk{z:euclid} The price of sorting is a finite fibre: a few non-isometric lattices share a key --- generically 30 for V1, 15 for V2, 4 for V4, none for V3 and V5 --- so equal keys are never taken as proof of identity.\chk{z:fibre}

The key is linear rather than built from Kurlin's root products $\sqrt{p_{ij}}$ for one reason. A root product is only H\"older-$\tfrac12$ at a vanishing conorm, which every wall produces, so two lattices $\delta$ apart near a wall separate as $\sqrt\delta$. On monoclinic cells $(50,70,60,90,\beta,90)$, the pairs $\beta=(90{+}\delta,\,90{+}2\delta)$ are $0.80$, $0.080$ and $0.008$\,\Ang\ apart in the linear key for $\delta=1^\circ$, $0.1^\circ$ and $0.01^\circ$, but $3.1$, $0.95$ and $0.30$\,\Ang\ in the root key; on the Scotty cells of McCoy \emph{et al.} (2026) the root key reverses NCDist's ranking of three pairs, which the linear key reproduces --- NCDist enters only by rank, its scale being internal to it.\chk{z:linear-key} Measurement noise is the stochastic form of the same cusp (Appendix~D).\chk{z:noise}

On a family within $\delta$ of one reference --- the serial-crystallography case --- the lower bound becomes an equality. If $\delta$ is below half the reference's smallest positive conorm and half its smallest conorm gap, every member reduces in one chamber with one ordering, key distances are the aligned distances pair by pair, and the whole pairwise geometry of $N$ cells costs $N$ reductions. Near-tied conorms break only the equality, leaving a bounded underestimate that one alignment per cell to the reference removes.\chk{z:isometry}

\subsection{Data sets and implementation}
\label{sec:data}

Archive-scale results use the unit cells of 206\,214 PDB entries. The lysozyme family comprises all hen egg-white lysozyme entries (UniProt P00698; 1372 entries, 1361 with a cell). The carbonic anhydrase benchmark is a supplied list of 1\,065 PDB codes. Per-crystal serial data are the CrystFEL stream for a $\beta$-lactamase XFEL data set (CXIDB entry 83; 14\,445 patterns, 12\,474 indexed crystals, space group $P6_3$, cell near $41.8\times41.8\times233$\,\Ang). Synthetic families are a monoclinic series ($a=120$, $b=189.1$\,\Ang, $\beta=91.2^\circ$, $c$ from 150 to 100\,\Ang) that passes through the pseudo-symmetric region around $a=c$, and 200 perturbed monoclinic frames straddling a reduction flip. All methods are implemented in the \texttt{agentsg} Python package, with the KD tree from SciPy (\texttt{scipy.spatial.cKDTree}); the pseudo-code of Appendix~E is sufficient to re-implement each step independently.

\subsection{Symmetry and pseudo-symmetry}
\label{sec:res-sym}
\label{sec:flip}

Matched over its closure, an orthorhombic P cell returns the order-8 $mmm$ coset against itself, and the $P6_3$ cell 24 operators ($6/mmm$ with $-I$); matched over one representative per class they return 2 and 4. Presented in an even-class basis, the orthorhombic cell returns the full coset where the 24 relabellings of one reduced superbase return nothing.\chk{z:coset}

The difference between a near-symmetry and a setting change is sharpest in a controlled monoclinic family. Exchanging $a$ and $c$ is an exact integer relation between two settings of the same lattice for every $|a-c|$, and an exact symmetry only at $a=c$. A $3^\circ$ Le~Page gate admits the exchange up to about $|a-c|=2.85$\,\Ang\ and then drops it, although the two descriptions remain exactly related; raising the tolerance merely admits unrelated pseudo-symmetries. The closure route returns the exact exchange at every distortion (Table~\ref{tab:flip}).\chk{z:lepage} Imposing $a=c$ by the constrained solve gives $H^*$ of index 2 over $H$, with the exchange carrying a residual proportional to $|a-c|$; the near-tetragonal cuboid with its two short edges equalised gives index 2 and a fourfold outside $H$.\chk{z:pseudo}

Measurement noise interacts with the band in the expected way. Too tight a band lets noise select a subset of the coset: on $P6_3$ frames with $0.05^\circ$ angular noise, a relative tolerance of $10^{-4}$ returned 12 of the 24 operators and $10^{-3}$ returned all 24.\chk{z:verify-tol} With exact zero detection a measured $P6_3$ frame is classified as V1 and only one superbase's relabellings are matched; widening restores the V4 classification and the full closure, and two independently perturbed frames at $0.01^\circ$ and $0.05^\circ$ angular noise both recovered all 24 operators.\chk{z:noisy-frames}

\subsection{Reindexing}
\label{sec:reindex}
\label{sec:tolerance}

On an arbitrary unimodular resetting of a reference cell, with and without noise and centering, the procedure recovers the resetting up to automorphism for eight lattice types.\chk{z:reindex-proc} On 200 perturbed monoclinic frames spanning both sides of a reduction flip, the key-first workflow reduced each frame to a cached two-operator coset, with the reference closure enumerated once.\chk{z:reindex-bench} A brute-force baseline testing all 6\,960 matrices per frame takes a median 15\,ms per frame on this machine and the cached procedure 75\,ms; earlier figures of 0.07\,ms per frame and a 330-fold speed-up are not reproduced and are not used. The baseline is a cost comparison, not a correctness reference: for arbitrary input settings it fails outright, since 192 of 200 random resettings with entries up to $\pm2$ are invisible to it.\chk{z:brute-complete}

The usual defence is to reduce both cells first. With Niggli reduction the defence works empirically: for two Niggli cells of the same lattice, exact or noisy, in random input settings, every relating operator had entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$ across eight lattice types and under adversarial anisotropic and near-degenerate resettings.\chk{z:brute-complete} With Selling reduction both halves of the question become provable, and they come apart. Finding \emph{one} operator always succeeds (Section~\ref{sec:closure}). Returning the \emph{coset} provably fails: the coset of a single Selling-reduced V4 or V5 cell against itself already contains operators with an entry of two (18 of 24 and 60 of 64 reduced bases), and two cuboid settings from different arrangement classes --- $(7.07,4,5,90,135,124.4)$ and $(6.40,4,5.83,90,132.0,128.7)$, both reduced presentations of the $3\times4\times5$ cuboid --- are related by an eight-operator coset of which the small list returns four and silently drops the rest, including an entry-three operator.\chk{z:brute-complete} The closure route is coset-complete unconditionally, by enumeration, with at most 32 candidates rather than 6\,960.

\subsection{Comparison under declared error, and one deformed family}
\label{sec:declared}
\label{sec:ca}

\emph{Declared error.} Take a Scotty base cell, reduce it, and perturb its metric by $\Delta\Gmet=D\,E$ as in Section~\ref{sec:deform}. Three facts emerge. First, the representative jumps long before the error is interesting: at $D=20$\,\Ang$^2$, three parts in a thousand of $\|\Gmet\|$, the Niggli basis of the 2yht cell lands in a different reduction chamber in more than half of random directions (14 of 25) --- the parameters stay continuous, but the operator relating the two Niggli frames stops being a signed permutation.\chk{z:declared-error} Second, both routes find the relation: the radius-one list finds a value at most $D$ in every run, and so does the widened closure at $\sqrt3\,D$.\chk{z:declared-error} Third, only one route can prove it. The list's proof is the anisotropy budget $D<4\lambda_{\min}-\lambda_{\max}$ of equation~(\ref{eq:cert}): $1\,739$\,\Ang$^2$ for 2yht, but \emph{negative} for seven of the seventeen cells (Table~\ref{tab:declared}) --- both lysozyme cells among them, with $\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}=5.5$ and a budget of $-1077$\,\Ang$^2$ for 2d4k. In fifteen perturbed runs on 2d4k the list recovered the relation every time and could certify it never, while the widened closure recovered it every time with its certificate intact.\chk{z:declared-error}

\emph{One protein, one deformed family.} A benchmark of 1\,065 carbonic anhydrase entries exercises the whole pipeline on one protein. Retrieval needed one query: every entry fell inside a single key ball of radius 2.5\,\Ang\ around 1CA2, the classic monoclinic cell $(42.7, 41.7, 73.0, 90, 104.6, 90)$.\chk{z:ca-benchmark} The set is one crystal form, continuously deformed: 1\,062 $P2_1$ cells and three $P1$ deposits of the same lattice shape, one key-connected component at radius 0.5\,\Ang, with nearest-neighbour key distances of median 0.05\,\Ang\ across 956 distinct deposited cells. The family medoid is 4L5V, with an anisotropy budget of about $1\,155$\,\Ang$^2$, and on seeded random pairs both the radius-one certificate and the margin certificate hold in every case: the closure minimum \emph{is} the ideal on this family, provably, pair by pair.\chk{z:ca-benchmark} Certified distances have median 82\,\Ang$^2$, strain norms median 1.2\% and 90th percentile 2.2\%, out to the farthest member 6BBS, a 10.3\% swollen copy of the same form at a certified 639\,\Ang$^2$.\chk{z:ca-benchmark} The same ball contains 145 further PDB entries not on the supplied list; proximity in lattice space, with no sequence information, assembled the family.

The deformation is low-dimensional and legible (Fig.~\ref{fig:ca}). Classical multidimensional scaling of the certified distances puts 84\% of the variance on one axis, and the strain tensors against the medoid put 79\% on one mode: the family is essentially a single swelling coordinate, volumetric strain from $-1\%$ to $+6\%$ with deviatoric strain near $0.5\%$, 1FQR the shear extreme and 2WEO the angle extreme ($\beta=109.5^\circ$).\chk{z:ca-benchmark} All 1\,065 Niggli cells occupy a single reduction chamber, even though isotropic errors a tenth this size jump chambers in half of all directions: jumps require crossing a wall, and this family's biological deformation runs parallel to its walls --- and close to one, the $a\!=\!b$ exchange at under 1\% of anisotropic strain away.\chk{z:ca-benchmark}

\subsection{What replaces what}

Table~\ref{tab:toolchain} sets the closure route against the classical one, task by task. In every row the classical tool rests on a tolerance or a fixed list with no completeness statement, and the closure route performs the task with a stated guarantee under one declared noise scale. The guarantees are not cleverness; they are what a set-valued reduction yields by construction. Selling reduction \emph{closes}: the set of reduced frames is finite, enumerable, and the same integer object for every lattice of a type, so statements about reduced settings reduce to one finite check per type. Niggli reduction \emph{selects} a single extremal representative, and has no closure equivalent: the nearest analogue, the set of Buerger cells, changes membership as the parameters move, chopping each stratum into the case structure that Gruber (1973) mapped by hand and Andrews \& Bernstein (2014) by Monte~Carlo search. Its boundary instability, its many-case boundary polytopes and the folklore entry bound for its operators are the cost of selecting one representative instead of keeping the closed set. What Niggli reduction keeps is convention, not capability: it is the standard way to report a cell, and its 44 lattice characters are a familiar lookup. For assigning the Bravais class the Selling side is the more natural route. The class is the zero-and-tie pattern of the reduced conorms --- Delone's classification into 24 sorts, which map onto the 14 Bravais types (Delone, 1932) --- and because each pattern is a list of linear constraints (Section~\ref{sec:symmetry}), membership is a constrained solve rather than a lookup among inequality-defined cases with their own boundary instabilities; the exact automorphism group comes from the closure self-match, and under noise the assignment carries a price in \Ang$^2$ instead of a tolerance on the characters.

\subsection{When comparison is hard}
\label{sec:hard}

Every instability of matching lives where its minimiser switches between branches, and there are exactly two kinds of place where that happens (Appendix~C). A \emph{wall} is near when the smallest positive conorm $m$ is within reach of the noise: below $\eta<m/3$ the plain closure is exact and needs no widening --- the carbonic anhydrase family lives here entirely --- and nearer than that the widening repairs it, with the guarantees of Section~\ref{sec:widening}. A \emph{tie} is near when two inequivalent alignments differ by less than $2\eta$, which is the pseudo-symmetry margin $s(B)$ of Section~\ref{sec:symmetry}: there no metric method can decide, and the honest output is the coset, handed to intensity statistics. Cell shape is not on the list. The eccentricity $\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ decides only whether a fixed matrix list carries a certificate --- it is a condition on that method, not on the problem.

Two boundaries keep the claim honest. Every guarantee is conditional on the declared $\eta$: an under-declared noise scale voids them silently, which is why $\eta$ is derived from the experiment or bootstrapped from a certified first pass ($\sqrt3\,D_0$) rather than set by hand. And deep ties are decided by intensity statistics or not at all; the contribution there is a loud, group-closed coset in place of a silent branch choice. Within these limits the record of the classical toolkit is explained rather than endorsed: most data are benign. On the carbonic anhydrase family every route agrees and always did; on seven of the seventeen benchmark cells the fixed list runs uncertified, small isotropic errors jump Niggli chambers in half of all directions, and a plain cuboid's coset escapes every entry-bounded list. The certificates cost two eigenvalues and six conorm comparisons per pair, and convert ``it usually works'' into ``it worked here, provably''.

\subsection{Scope and proposed experiments}

Three objects stay distinct: Kurlin's root form, which is the complete isometry classification; the sorted key, which is continuous and Euclidean but many-to-one; and the closure match, which establishes identity. The framework addresses equality, similarity and basis equivalence of primitive lattices; sub- and supercell relations change the lattice and need finite-index enumeration when they are part of the problem.

The closure makes several measurements well-posed, each a bounded computation on public data. (i)~\emph{A hardness census}: the margin, the conorm gap and the first self-spectrum rungs for all 206\,214 PDB cells, attaching to each deposit its distances to every nearby Bravais class in \Ang$^2$. (ii)~\emph{Twinnability from the ladder}: correlate rung heights with deposited twin refinements and twin fractions, testing whether a price in \Ang$^2$ predicts twinning better than the obliquity gate it replaces. (iii)~\emph{A serial field test}: key, cluster, closure and coset on the $\beta$-lactamase stream and a second, multi-form stream, scored as certified, uncertified and coset fractions per frame against an intensity-based ambiguity resolver. (iv)~\emph{Noise calibration}: estimate $\eta$ from redundant depositions and repeated indexings, and measure the anisotropy of real error --- the one assumption the isotropic widening makes. (v)~\emph{A retrieval head-to-head}: recall at guaranteed radius on planted perturbations, a quantity an ordinal score cannot state. (vi)~\emph{Bravais assignment by price}: assign the class from the cheapest constraint list and compare with tolerance-based assigners on borderline entries, each disagreement localising, in \Ang$^2$, a cell where an angular gate misleads.

\section{Conclusions}
%==============================================================================

One finite object does the work of a toolchain. The Selling closure of a lattice --- every obtuse superbase, with the integer changes of basis between them --- is complete, enumerable and, widened to a declared noise scale, stable under measurement. Matched with another closure it gives the distance between lattices, certified, with the full coset of relating operators; matched with itself it gives the exact symmetry, the pseudo-symmetries in group-closed cosets, and the price of each in the data's unit; its constraint lists give the Bravais classes and, stacked, the nexus points where they meet; and sorted, it gives a Euclidean key on which the Protein Data Bank becomes an atlas. The angular symmetry gate and the fixed matrix list are not refined here but superseded: every operator either carries a residual in \Ang$^2$ or is provably absent. In one line: enumerate the closure; match it to compare, to reindex, and to find symmetry with its price; sort it to search.

\section{Selling reduction, the closure and its operators}
\label{app:closure}

\subsection{Superbase, conorms, vonorms}
In the $S^6$ convention of Andrews \emph{et al.} (2019) the Selling scalars are $s_{ij}=-p_{ij}$ and occupy the non-positive orthant. The seven vonorms (Conway \& Sloane, 1992) are the squared lengths of the four vectors and of the three opposite-edge partial sums,
\begin{equation}
v_i^2=\sum_{j\ne i}p_{ij},\qquad v_{ij}^2=(v_i+v_j)^2=p_{ik}+p_{il}+p_{jk}+p_{jl},
\end{equation}
with $\{k,l\}=\{0,1,2,3\}\setminus\{i,j\}$; they satisfy $v_0^2+v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2=v_{01}^2+v_{02}^2+v_{03}^2$ and are the $D_7$ descriptor of Andrews and Bernstein and Kurlin's voform.

\subsection{Reduction}
If some $p_{ij}=-\epsilon<0$, the Delone--Selling move replaces the superbase by
\begin{equation}
u_i=-v_i,\quad u_j=v_j,\quad u_k=v_i+v_k,\quad u_l=v_i+v_l,
\end{equation}
which keeps the zero sum, generates the same lattice, permutes six of the seven vonorms and decreases the seventh by $4\epsilon$; the sum of the six conorms decreases by $\epsilon$ (Kurlin, 2026, Lemma~A.1).\chk{z:reduction-step} Applying the move to the most negative conorm until none remains terminates in finitely many steps.\chk{z:reduction-step} This is the algorithm of Andrews \& Bernstein (1988, 2014) and Andrews \emph{et al.} (2019); the conventional-to-primitive conversion that precedes it is the only rational step.

\subsection{The closure and the type rule}
Kurlin's Lemmas~4.1--4.5 show that the relabellings of one obtuse superbase are all of them only for lattices whose Voronoi cell is the generic truncated octahedron (V1); for the four degenerate types the additional members arise by the move above at a conorm that is exactly zero. Table~\ref{tab:closure} lists the counts, confirmed by exhaustive enumeration of all obtuse superbases with bounded integer coordinates for one test lattice of each type.\chk{z:closure-count} The Voronoi type is read from the zero pattern of the reduced conorms: no zeros is V1; one is V2; two on opposite tetrahedral edges is V3; two sharing a vertex is V4; three is V5, and other three-zero patterns cannot occur.\chk{z:type-rule} For a symmetric lattice within a type the classes may coincide (the hexagonal lattice has one class rather than three); the enumeration handles this automatically.\chk{z:closure-count}

\subsection{Relation to the $S^6$ orbit and boundary transformations}
In the $S^6$ formulation a reduced lattice is a point in the non-positive orthant, and relabelling acts as a 24-element group of coordinate permutations. The closure is generally larger than one orbit: it is the full preimage of the lattice in the orthant. In $S^6$ the closure has 1, 2, 3, 6 and 16 distinct points for V1--V5 (global negation leaves scalars unchanged), falling into 1, 2, 3, 3 and 4 relabelling orbits.\chk{z:closure-count} Orbit and closure coincide only for V1, and the difference is operational: matched over one orbit, an orthorhombic P cell in an even-class basis returns no operator at all, while matched over the closure it returns the full $mmm$ coset.\chk{z:coset} The boundary transformations of Andrews \emph{et al.} (2019) at a face $s_i=0$ are the Selling moves at a zero scalar, which are exactly the moves that generate the extra members; the closure is the relabelling orbit together with its images under those transformations, iterated until closed, applied once per lattice with the integer bookkeeping that scalar points cannot hold, and complete by Kurlin's enumeration.

\subsection{The stratum proposition}
\emph{Proposition.} (i)~On each Voronoi stratum the closure, expressed in the integer coordinates of one obtuse superbase, is a fixed finite set --- 1, 2, 3, 6 and 16 classes modulo relabelling and sign for V1 through V5 --- each member obtuse on the entire open stratum, because every conorm of every member is a non-negative integer combination of the stratum's free conorms. (ii)~Between any two bases drawn from these superbases, the relating coset contains an operator with entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$. (iii)~It is not contained in them: in V4, 18 of the 24 reduced bases have a self-coset operator with an entry of two; in V5, 60 of 64, and bases from different arrangement classes are related by coset operators with an entry of three. \emph{Proof.} The metric is linear in the conorms (equation~\ref{eq:gram}), so whether a slot of a candidate superbase vanishes identically on the stratum is read off an integer coefficient vector, not off parameter values; the flip graph grown from the standard superbase by flips at identically-zero slots is therefore the same for every lattice of the type, and one breadth-first enumeration per type covers them all. Signed permutations of either basis multiply the relating operators on the outside and cannot change entry magnitudes, and a larger point group at a special lattice only enlarges the coset, so (ii) extends to every lattice. The finitely many operators are then listed and their entries read off.\chk{z:brute-complete} In V1 through V3 every coset operator stays inside $\{-1,0,1\}$; the escape is a V4 and V5 phenomenon, driven by skew member bases conjugating the point group.

\subsection{The change-of-basis web}
With $\Cmat_i$ the matrix whose columns are three vectors of member $i$ in the input basis, the change of basis from member $i$ to member $j$ is $\Wop_{ij}=\Cmat_i^{-1}\Cmat_j$, integer and unimodular because both factors are. The composition rule $\Wop_{ij}\Wop_{jk}=\Wop_{ik}$ and the inverse rule follow at once. If $\Pop$ maps the lattice onto itself, or one lattice's closure onto another's, it maps obtuse superbases to obtuse superbases, so it permutes the members, $\Pop\Cmat_i=\Cmat_{\sigma(i)}$ up to relabelling, and then $\Wop'_{\sigma(i)\sigma(j)}=\Pop\,\Wop_{ij}\,\Pop^{-1}$. On the worked cell the twofold along $b$ permutes the six members as $(1\,2)(3\,4)(5\,6)$ and the identity holds for every member pair.\chk{z:transport}

\section{Widening and measured data}
\label{app:widening}

\subsection{Overlap and clipping}
\emph{Proposition.} Let $\|\Gmet'-\Gmet\|_F\le\eta$ in a shared frame. (i)~Every member of either lattice's exact closure is, at the same integer coordinates, a member of the other's widened closure at $\tau=3\eta$: member coordinates have entries in $\{-1,0,1\}$ (Appendix~A), so each conorm moves by at most $\|w_i\|\|w_j\|\,\eta\le3\eta$. (ii)~Every frame in the widened set is an exact obtuse superbase of an explicitly constructed lattice within $2\sqrt6\,\tau$: clip its negative conorms to zero; the clipped metric moves by at most $2\tau$ per slot, the frame is obtuse for it by construction, and Kurlin's completeness certifies it as a closure member of that lattice.\chk{z:overlap} A reduction move at a slightly negative slot and a widening flip at a slightly positive one are the same integer move, so the widened closure is one breadth-first closure under band-limited Selling moves; because a flip shifts neighbouring conorms by up to twice its size, a rigorous band for the walk is $2\tau$. The machine check verifies both directions across all five Voronoi types.\chk{z:overlap}

\subsection{Zero tolerance and serial noise}
Angular error is approximately Gaussian in conorm space, because $p_{ij}$ is linear in the metric: $\sigma_{p_{ij}}\approx|v_i||v_j|\sigma_\theta$.\chk{z:noise} The tolerance that decides which conorms count as zero must be closure-invariant. Per-pair floors $|v_i||v_j|\sigma_\theta$ are not: the even-class superbases of a cuboid have different edge lengths, and keys computed from two bases of the same cuboid then differ by about 0.02\,\Ang.\chk{z:floor-invariance} The invariant scale is $T=\sum p_{ij}=\tfrac12\sum_i|v_i|^2$, and the floor is $s=\sigma_\theta T$. A declared angular error $\sigma_\theta$ (and length error, if wanted) is converted once into $\eta$ on this scale, and every tolerance downstream is a stated multiple of it. Floored and soft-threshold root maps, $\sqrt{p+s}-\sqrt s$ and $\sqrt{\max(p-\kappa s,0)}$, are noise models for frame-level serial data, not archive keys; Table~\ref{tab:noise} gives their measured behaviour.\chk{z:stabilise}

\subsection{The guarantee under the noise model}
Under the congruence hypothesis the chain of Section~\ref{sec:reindex-proc} is provable step by step. \emph{Retrieval}: the linear key is Lipschitz in the metric, so two measurements of one lattice have key distance of order $\eta$, and a radius at a stated multiple of $\eta$ retrieves the pair; Lemma~\ref{lem:lb} makes the radius sufficient. \emph{Coverage}: by the overlap proposition, the widened closure of each measured cell contains a counterpart of every member of the true closure, with integer provenance.\chk{z:guarantee} \emph{Coset}: for each true operator the matched counterparts are present, its entries are bounded, and its residual on the measured metrics is of order $\eta$; a band at the stated multiple accepts the full coset, and the partial coset of Section~\ref{sec:res-sym} is what a band below it produces. \emph{Soundness}: acceptance requires an exact integer map carrying one measured metric onto the other within the band, and with exact data only true operators satisfy it.\chk{z:guarantee} The constants are small and computable, and the Supporting Information verifies the chain across the five Voronoi types together with its failure when a tolerance is set below its multiple.

\section{Matching, cosets and symmetry}
\label{app:match}

\subsection{The ideal distance}
Two declarations enter equation~(\ref{eq:ideal}): the norm (Frobenius on the metric) and the frame in which $\Delta\Gmet$ is measured ($A$'s reported reduced frame). $B$ enters only as a lattice, because re-presenting it maps $\Mop\mapsto N\Mop$ and reparametrises the set, while $A$ enters through its frame, which the symmetrised maximum balances. The set of candidate values is discrete and locally finite, so the minimum is attained. For a face-straddling pair --- one lattice with a conorm at $+\delta$, the other from the same frame with that conorm at $-\delta$ --- the minimiser moves one angle by $0.85^\circ$ and essentially nothing else, and the distance is exactly the $4\delta$ that separates the pair.\chk{z:deformation}

\subsection{Near-obtuseness and the eigenvalue certificate}
A candidate achieving value $D$ has frame conorms, read in $A$'s reduced coordinates, that differ from $A$'s non-negative ones by bilinear forms of the residual, each bounded by $\sqrt3\,D$; the minimising frame therefore penetrates the obtuse cone by at most $\sqrt3\,D$, with observed worst case $0.70\,D$ over random reduced pairs.\chk{z:deformation} Equation~(\ref{eq:cert}) is an independent certificate from the eigenvalue side: every entry of $\Mop$ is bounded by $\|\Mop\|_2$, so any improver lies in the entry ball of radius $R(D)$, and because the running minimum only shrinks, the ball at the seed's radius already contains every improver. It is certified on the Hfq pair, where $R=1.86$; on a reduced needle pair with $R\approx7$, the radius-two ball improves the radius-one minimum by a factor of 2.8, so the truncated list returns a wrong ideal there, as the certificate warned;\chk{z:brute-complete} and on the $P6_3$ cell $R\approx7.9$ already at $D=0$, where the entry ball is infeasible and the widened closure is the enumerator.\chk{z:deformation} The constraint is in fact anisotropic: each column of $\Mop$ must be a lattice vector of $B$ whose length matches the corresponding cell edge of $A$ to within $D$, so the search is three thin ellipsoidal shells, not a cube of digits. On the $P6_3$ needle against itself at $D=50$\,\Ang$^2$ the shells hold 6, 6 and 2 candidate vectors, 72 column triples, against $15^9\approx4\times10^{10}$ matrices in the entry box; widening to $D\approx125$\,\Ang$^2$ admits the in-plane stacks of length 232.7\,\Ang\ that shadow the 233\,\Ang\ axis.\chk{z:declared-error}

\subsection{The closure match is a congruence test, not a metric}
Between genuinely different lattices the plain closure minimum is an upper bound on the frame infimum, and not a tight one: it is discontinuous across walls. For the face-straddling pair above the key distance is of order $\delta$ while the closure minimum stays near $0.51$ as $\delta\to0$, an overestimate by factors 3, 14 and 71 at $\delta=0.5$, $0.1$ and $0.02$, because the two reduced forms land in different arrangements.\chk{z:closure-metric} On the Hfq pair 4nl7$\to$2yht, key and closure minimum agree at $5.43$\,\Ang, while widening lowers the match to $2.83$ and then $2.65$\,\Ang\ --- the verdict NCDist reaches for this pair (McCoy \emph{et al.}, 2026), compared by rank only. If the closure minimum is smaller than every strictly positive normalised conorm of both lattices, no frame within that distance can cross a wall, and the closure minimum is the frame infimum; in 40 random interior pairs, widening improved the minimum only where a lattice margin lay below it.\chk{z:closure-metric}

\subsection{Alignment ambiguity is a coset}
Define $\mathrm{Aut}_\eta(B)=\{Q\in\mathrm{GL}(3,\mathbb Z):\|Q^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_B Q-\Gmet_B\|\le\eta\}$. \emph{Proposition.} If $\Pop_0$ aligns the closures of $A$ and $B$ within $\eta$ of optimal, the set of all such alignments is the single coset $\Pop_0\,\mathrm{Aut}_{2\eta}(B)$: for two near-optimal alignments, $Q=\Pop_1^{-1}\Pop_2$ is a $2\eta$-automorphism by the triangle inequality, and conversely composing $\Pop_0$ with one stays in the band. The alignment is unique exactly when $2\eta$ lies below $s(B)=\min_{Q\notin\mathrm{Aut}(B)}\|Q^{\mathsf T}\Gmet_B Q-\Gmet_B\|$, the first nonzero value of the self-spectrum. The returned band is checked for group closure --- every $\Pop_i^{-1}\Pop_j$ must itself lie within twice the band --- which certifies that no coset was cut in half.\chk{z:align-coset} When two inequivalent alignments differ by less than $2\eta$ the branch choice is ill-posed for every metric-only method; completeness turns that from a silent error into a reported coset.

\subsection{The price of a symmetry}
A target class is a list of linear constraints on the conorms --- walls $p_{ij}=0$ and ties $p_{ij}=p_{kl}$, imposed on whole relabelling orbits, otherwise the imposed group can fail to contain the exact one --- and the nearest metric satisfying the list is the least-squares solution of the stacked system. \emph{Proposition.} For an involution $Q$, the map $\Gmet\mapsto Q^{\mathsf T}\Gmet Q$ is an isometric reflection of metric space, so its nearest fixed metric is $\tfrac12(\Gmet+Q^{\mathsf T}\Gmet Q)$ and the residual is exactly twice the distance to $Q$'s fixed stratum. Crystallographic point operators are involutions or products of them, and in general $\max r/2\le\mathrm{dist}\le\mathrm{mean}\,r$. The worked numbers of Section~\ref{sec:symmetry} are verified, including the reflection identity to machine precision on every accepted operator.\chk{z:sym-profile} Stacking two classes' lists and solving gives their common solutions, positive-definite at a nexus and singular at a seam; the machine check derives every entry of Table~\ref{tab:nexus} that way from constraint lists read off generic reduced cells of each family.\chk{z:nexus}

\subsection{Pseudo-symmetry by stacked constraints}
\begin{small}\begin{verbatim}
PSEUDO_SYMMETRY(ref, delta):           # once per reference; delta in A^2
  cf <- conorms(ref.U_R, ref.G_R)      # integer coordinates retained
  H  <- ref.H                          # exact Aut(R) from the self-match
  patterns <- zero sets {p_ij < delta} and tie sets {|p_ij - p_kl| < delta},
              extended to Aut(R)-orbits, and their unions
  out <- {}
  for pat in patterns:
      G*  <- least-squares metric satisfying pat   # constrained solve
      d   <- ||G - G*||;  if d > delta: continue
      S*  <- closure(ref.U_R, G*);  H* <- self-match of G* over S*
      assert H subset H*
      for P in H* \ H: residual(P) <- ||P^T G P - G||   # = 2 d for involutions
      out <- out + {(pat, d, cosets H*/H, cosets H*/L, residuals)}
  return out
\end{verbatim}\end{small}
The checks reported with this paper impose two strata by hand --- $a=c$ on the monoclinic family and equal short edges on the near-tetragonal cuboid --- and the worked $mmm$ case of Section~\ref{sec:symmetry}.\chk{z:pseudo}

\subsection{Relation to Lebedev's coset reduction}
Lebedev \emph{et al.} (2006) reduce the operators that intensity statistics must examine to representatives of the cosets of the Laue group $L$ in the lattice group $H$. For index 2 the non-trivial coset always contains a twofold; for higher index it need not (the coset $\{4,4^3\}$ of $2$ in $422$ contains none), so representatives are chosen of minimal order.\chk{z:coset-reps} Their $H$ comes from a tolerance search; here $H=\mathrm{Aut}(R)$ is exact, and the same argument applied to the priced $H^*$ gives the pseudo-merohedral branches in a separate, declared step.

\section{The sorted key}
\label{app:key}

\subsection{One key per lattice}
Relabelling permutes the six conorms, so the sorted list is unchanged, and central inversion leaves every conorm unchanged. For the additional members at V2--V5, Kurlin's Lemmas~4.2--4.5 give each alternative coform as a permutation of the reduced one; hence the multiset of conorms, and so $\sortkey$, is the same on every member. This is the modern form of the classical statement that the reduced Selling scalars are unique as a list (Bravais, 1850; Delone, 1932; Andrews \emph{et al.}, 2019). Numerically, the 4, 6, 12 and 32 superbases of the V2--V5 test lattices each gave a single key.\chk{z:one-key}

\subsection{Continuity}
Sorting is 1-Lipschitz. Across a Voronoi-type boundary the reduction may return a superbase near a different closure member, but all members share the limiting key, so the lattice-level key is continuous through the boundary.\chk{z:trajectory}

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:lb}}
Expanding $\|x-\sigma y\|_2^2=\|x\|^2+\|y\|^2-2\langle x,\sigma y\rangle$, the minimum over $\sigma$ is attained when $\langle x,\sigma y\rangle$ is maximal, which by the rearrangement inequality occurs when both vectors are similarly ordered; that value is $\langle\sort x,\sort y\rangle$. The inequality holds because $G\subseteq S_6$.\chk{z:lowerbound} With $G$ the image of $S_4$ in $S_6$ (order 24), the key distance is the tightest lower bound on the orbit distance available from the unpaired multiset alone.

\subsection{Why the key is Euclidean and the orbit metric is not}
The lemma identifies the key distance with the quotient metric of $\mathbb R^6$ by $S_6$. $S_6$ is generated by transpositions, each a reflection in a hyperplane $x_i=x_j$, and the quotient by a finite reflection group is isometric to a closed Weyl chamber, here the convex cone $\{s_1\le\dots\le s_6\}$ with the flat metric. The image of $S_4$ contains no reflection --- a transposition of two vertices moves two pairs of edges, a rotation in $\mathbb R^6$ --- so its quotient is an orbifold with singular strata, and the orbit metric is not Euclidean-embeddable, as Schoenberg's criterion confirms numerically.\chk{z:euclid} This is the obstruction a metric on Kurlin's invariant space must address; the key sidesteps it by quotienting by the larger reflection group, at the price of the collisions counted next.

\subsection{Collisions}
The key forgets the pairing of conorms into the three opposite-edge columns of Kurlin's $2\times3$ root form. For six distinct values the $S_4$-orbit of a labelled coform has 24 elements among $6!=720$ arrangements, so 30 non-isometric lattices share a generic V1 key; the same count gives 15 for V2 ($5!/|D_4|$) and 4 for V4, while V3 and V5 are injective. Concretely, the coforms $\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\4&5&6\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\4&6&5\end{pmatrix}$ are non-isometric lattices with identical keys.\chk{z:fibre} Appending the sorted square roots of the seven vonorms separates every generic V1 fibre in 300 random trials, an observation rather than a theorem; the six-dimensional key remains the archive key.\chk{z:d7}

\subsection{When the key is exact: one-chamber families}
Let the reference have margin $m$ (smallest positive normalised conorm) and gap $g$ (smallest spacing between distinct conorms), and let every member deviate by less than $\delta<\min(m,g)/2$. Then every member reduces with the same conorm ordering, sorting is one fixed permutation, key distances equal aligned distances for every pair, the fibre is trivial on the family, and the margin certificate holds on every pair. When only the gap condition fails, sorting may swap a near-tied pair and the key underestimates by a bounded slack, never overestimates; aligning each member once to the cached reference restores exactness at the same $O(N)$ cost.\chk{z:isometry}

\subsection{The H\"older cusp of root products}
Where $p>0$ the square root has slope $1/(2\sqrt p)$; where $p=0$ it satisfies only $|\sqrt x-\sqrt y|\le\sqrt{|x-y|}$, so its response to noise scales as $\sqrt{\sigma_p}$. On the $P6_3$ cell $(41.8,41.8,233,90,90,120)$ a $0.01^\circ$ angular error moves the root key by a median 1.58\,\Ang\ (Table~\ref{tab:noise}), ten times the $c$-axis spread of the XFEL stream, against roughly 0.03\,\Ang\ on a generic triclinic cell; the linear key moves by 0.01\,\Ang.\chk{z:noise}

\section{The reindexing procedure}
\label{app:exact}

\subsection{Operator construction}
During reduction the four superbase vectors are tracked as integer coordinate triples in the input primitive basis, for every closure member. For a candidate pair let $\Uop(A)$ and $\Uop(B)$ be $3\times3$ integer matrices whose columns are three of the four vectors of a member of $\Sclosure(A)$ and $\Sclosure(B)$, under one relabelling and one sign. Any three vectors of a zero-sum superbase form a primitive basis, so both are unimodular and $\Pop=\Uop(A)\Uop(B)^{-1}$ is an exact integer matrix, accepted if $\Pop^{\mathsf T}\Gmet(A)\Pop=\Gmet(B)$ within the band $\max(\texttt{verify\_abs},\ \texttt{verify\_rel}\cdot\tr|\Gmet(B)|)$. The residual is linear in the metric, so its noise model is Gaussian with scale $\sigma_p$.

\subsection{Pseudo-code}
\begin{small}\begin{verbatim}
PREPARE_REFERENCE(R_conv, sigma):           # run once, cache the result
  C_R, G_R <- centering matrix and primitive metric of R   # rational C_R
  eta      <- noise scale from sigma on the invariant scale T(G_R)
  U_R      <- selling_reduce(G_R)            # integer rows, obtuse
  S_R      <- widened_closure(U_R, G_R, 3*eta)   # all members, <= 32 exact
  key_R    <- sort(conorms / sqrt(T));  radius <- multiple of eta
  H        <- REINDEX_FRAME(ref, R_conv)     # self-match: Aut(R)
  if space group known: branches <- one representative per coset H/L
  return ref

REINDEX_FRAME(ref, N_conv):                  # per frame
  C_N, G_N <- centering matrix and primitive metric of N
  if |key(G_N) - ref.key_R| > ref.radius: return EMPTY     # optional
  S_N <- widened_closure(selling_reduce(G_N), G_N, 3*eta)  # usually 1
  ops <- {}
  for C_R' in ref.S_R, C_N' in S_N, pi in S4, s in {+1,-1}:
      P <- (rows 1..3 of s*C_R'[pi])^T (rows 1..3 of C_N')^{-T}
      if || P^T ref.G_R P - G_N || <= band(eta): ops <- ops + {P}
  ops <- deduplicate(ops)                    # = P0 * ref.H, by det sign
  # x_R = P x_N ; h_R = P^{-T} h_N ; W_R = P W_N P^{-1} ; P_conv = C_R P C_N^{-1}
  return ops
\end{verbatim}\end{small}

\subsection{Niggli-first and bounded searches}
Between Niggli cells of one lattice the entry bound holds empirically for the whole coset, because the Niggli basis is unique and axis-aligned, so the coset is the point group up to signed permutations.\chk{z:brute-complete} It stays empirical for a structural reason: Niggli reduction has no closure equivalent, so no single finite enumeration settles it, and shortness alone cannot substitute --- the cuboid example of Section~\ref{sec:reindex} relates bases built entirely from short vectors by an entry of three. Niggli-first therefore makes the small list coset-complete in practice, at the price of reintroducing the representative's boundary behaviour and testing 6\,960 candidates where the closure needs at most 32. The implementation of the Niggli change of basis must itself be verified: the returned matrix $N$ should satisfy $N^{\mathsf T}\Gmet N=\Gmet_{\rm red}$ exactly.\chk{z:brute-complete}

\subsection{If nothing is accepted}
Either $N$ is a different lattice; or $N$ is a sub- or supercell of $R$, requiring finite-index enumeration outside this procedure; or the band is tighter than the noise. The residual of the best candidate is reported in every case, so the third possibility is visible rather than silent.
